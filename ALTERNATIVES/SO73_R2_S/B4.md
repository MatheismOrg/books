## Overview

Everything is either ruled or random. Both are mathematical. Nothing outside mathematical structure can exist, because consciousness must be structure to interact with structure, and even the concept of something non-mathematical is itself a mathematical structure. This book develops the rules-random dichotomy into a necessity argument: not merely that physics uses math, but that nothing else is possible.

Necessity says what exists: all consistent math. Simplicity says where a mind is likely to find itself within it. The Simplicity Dominance Principle gives shorter complete descriptions exponentially greater weight: each additional bit halves the measure. Together they settle the old puzzles. Fine-tuning is resolved without design; all consistent mathematical structures exist, and observers find themselves in structures that support observation. Forward time follows from description economy: an endless future has no last frame to reverse from, and specifying a reversal point costs the bits needed to locate it. The block universe is the mathematics, not a container for it. Complete mathematical causality makes superdeterminism possible: measurement settings and outcomes share one history.

The argument engages the physicist Max Tegmark's mathematical universe hypothesis and the computer scientist Jürgen Schmidhuber's computable universe, arguing both stop short of necessity. They catalog mathematical existence without closing every exit. This argument closes them: the consciousness exit, the randomness exit, and the question of why mathematical truth requires no further act of creation to be real. The road to that proof is older than either of them, and it starts in the dark.

## The Path to Proof

The light of one math could have been seen in total darkness. Thales, who sought one substance. Anaximander, the boundless. Heraclitus, logos (the rational order behind all change). Democritus, atoms and void. The Pythagorean tradition put number at the heart of reality some 2,500 years ago. Mathematical reality did not begin with Tegmark, Einstein, Leibniz, Newton, Spinoza, or even Pythagoras.

Pythagoras was on the right track. Then Socrates dragged Plato and the youth back from the one abstract truth to dialectic; Cicero said he called philosophy down from the heavens. Plato divided the changing world from eternal Forms. Spinoza said one substance. Leibniz demanded a sufficient reason: why this rather than otherwise? Mathematical necessity is the reason. A chooser would need a nature too.

Kant dragged philosophy back into the cave. His Critique of Pure Reason distinguished appearances from things-in-themselves: what we can know from what we cannot know as it is independently of us. Space and time became forms of our intuition, the built-in frames of all perception. The wall does not survive Matheism. Whatever exists has a nature; whatever has a nature has structure. Calling it unknowable does not make it nonmathematical. We feel a subset of reality from inside. There is no second substance hiding behind the phenomenon. The map is the territory.

The scientific convergence ran in parallel. Galileo read nature in mathematics. Newton’s Principia Mathematica gave motion and gravitation mathematical laws in 1687, three centuries before Tegmark’s ensemble paper. Riemann developed the geometry Einstein would need. Boltzmann connected entropy, a measure of disorder, to probability. Cantor showed that infinities have different sizes. Einstein and Minkowski joined space and time. Gödel found limits to what sufficiently strong, consistent, effectively axiomatized systems can prove. Turing defined computation and its limits. Shannon gave information a mathematical measure. Each sharpened the tools. None of those results, by itself, proves that nothing outside mathematics can exist. That is the exit to close.

Randomness looked like the other exit, and its math began at the gaming table. The French mathematicians Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat were working out the mathematics of chance in 1654. Pascal later wagered on belief in God. Flip the wager. A God worth anything would want to see you were not gullible, that you did not kneel for a bribe or believe nonsense for a reward. If some tester in the structure is watching at all, it watches whether you loved the one truth over the comfortable story. The wager favors the refusal to be fooled. Truth is not a vote.

After probability, after Newton, it got easier and easier. Even the supposed escape through randomness led into mathematics. Kolmogorov gave probability its axiomatic foundation in 1933. His 1965 work on description length helped make compressibility itself mathematical: how short a program can produce a given string? These are different achievements. A random string does not acquire a soul by resisting compression. Nor does a probability distribution create the outcomes it describes. Ruled or random, both exits lead to math.

Schmidhuber’s manuscript, dated December 1996 and published in 1997, already considered all computable universes and their algorithmic information, the length of the shortest program that produces each. Tegmark’s ensemble preprint followed in April 1997, proposing physical existence for all mathematical structures. Schmidhuber later developed a speed prior, weighting computational cost as well as description; the dedicated paper appeared in 2002. Tegmark’s 2007 paper, published in 2008, explicitly added a computable-universe hypothesis. It cited Schmidhuber’s earlier work. Computability was in Schmidhuber’s account from the start.

Tegmark did more than collect equations: he discussed self-aware structures and why mathematical existence should count as physical existence. Matheism presses the demand for necessity. Why can nothing else exist? Why does consciousness offer no escape? Why would a mathematical world need something outside mathematics to make it real? Showing that physics uses math does not close those exits. The argument must.

The physicist John Wheeler said "it from bit": information is fundamental, reality arises from measurement. He put the observer where necessity should be. His participatory universe says observers create reality by measuring it. Math does not need an observer to exist. 2+2=4 whether anyone measures it. When the obvious question hit, who observed the first observer, he pointed to delayed-choice experiments, where the measurement is chosen after the particle is in flight, and said observers can retroactively shape the past. That is not closing the loop. That is drawing the loop and calling it a feature.

Schmidhuber's speed prior weights structures by computational cost: fewer steps, higher weight. Description length measures what a structure is. Speed measures how long it takes to produce. If mathematical structures exist without being computed, description length matters and speed does not. He is measuring the cost of a construction project for a building that was never built; it just exists. Speed matters inside a simulation, where the simulator's budget constrains what gets run. But the simulator's universe still has to exist, and that existence is mathematical necessity, not computation. At the base level, nothing is being run. Schmidhuber's speed prior is a local truth nested in a global one: the Simplicity Dominance Principle applies to existence itself.

He explored structures beyond ordinary computation, but left the full uncountable landscape, too vast for any endless list, outside his framework.

The proof was always available, one sentence away: everything is ruled or random, both are math, so there is no third option, and every interacting dualism requires one shared structure. Pythagoras could have closed it. Or a caveman in the dark shadow of any cave. Or a three-eyed raven, or a counting bee. Bees can learn to place an empty display below displays with one or more objects: even zero enters their numerical order. The bee need not know the word. We had the words. What took us so long?

## From Simple Laws to Experience

Which structures support minds, and why do we find these laws? The Simplicity Dominance Principle gives shorter complete descriptions more weight: every additional bit halves it. Count the laws and starting conditions together. From this I examine forward time; many-worlds and its branching quantum state; superdeterministic models with correlated measurement settings and hidden variables; variational physics, where classical motion makes the action stationary; and higher geometric dimensions. These require distinct derivations. The question is how much one principle can explain without adding independent assumptions.

All branches share one evolution. One quantum state, the full mathematical description of a system, includes the particle, the apparatus and the observer. A collapse, a sudden jump to one definite outcome, adds a rule; it must explain something the unbroken evolution does not. Count the whole account, including the probabilities of what we observe.

### Why the world looks classical

Send particles one at a time toward two slits. Each leaves a localized mark on the screen, but many marks build an interference pattern. The wave describes the alternatives and how their amplitudes combine; the localized detection does not mean the system was a tiny classical ball with a known route all along.

Now let a detector, an air molecule or scattered light carry a record distinguishing the routes. The combined state correlates the left route with one environmental record and the right route with another. Those correlations are entanglement. When the records are sufficiently distinguishable, measurements of the particle alone no longer reveal interference between the alternatives. [A double-slit model of decoherence](https://www.nist.gov/publications/measurement-induced-decoherence-and-information-double-slit-interference) makes this distinction explicit. Nobody has to read the record or be conscious of it.

The information has spread into correlations with the surroundings. It has not simply vanished from the full quantum state. Restoring interference would require control over the relevant correlations; for a macroscopic object continually scattering light and colliding with its environment, that rapidly becomes impractical.

Why a path? Repeated interactions favor states that retain stable records, often localized wave packets. Under suitable conditions, their centers move approximately according to classical equations. Localization and the dynamics work together. A path is a useful large-scale approximation, not a wave being squeezed into a permanently classical bead. [Zurek's account of environmental selection](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0105127) explains how such stable states emerge.

Decoherence therefore explains the loss of accessible interference. By itself it does not select one unique outcome for the whole universe. In [Everett's no-collapse account](https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.29.454), the full state continues evolving, with different records in effectively separate branches. Each observer's record is definite within its branch. Only alternatives supported by the state and dynamics occur; many-worlds is not a promise that every imaginable story happens.

Superdeterminism addresses a different issue. Bell's argument uses statistical independence between measurement settings and the hidden variables of the measured system. [Superdeterministic models relax that assumption](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physics/articles/10.3389/fphy.2020.00139/full). Sharing a causal past is not enough by itself: the model must produce the particular correlations observed. This supplies no automatic collapse mechanism. Ordinary many-worlds does not need this hidden-variable route, and combining the labels does not supply a theory.

Discreteness answers neither question automatically. [Loop quantum gravity derives discrete area and volume spectra](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/9411005) within its mathematical framework; that is not experimental proof of universal space and time pixels. A discrete model may have quantum superpositions, or it may propose a single underlying state. Its equations must decide.

My preference is for a causally complete mathematical account. The work is to specify it, recover the successful predictions and identify a test that distinguishes it from alternatives. Environmental records explain classical appearance without waiting for that final account.

One mathematical quantum system can also advance every finite program, each through every finite stage it reaches. My construction gives it a reversible clock and unbounded time and memory. If the computations feel, it contains those computable minds. The construction does not need a separate universe for each program.

Cantor built the infinite hierarchy all modern math stands on. Gödel didn't find the limit of math. Point a camera at every corner of a room. It photographs everything except its own lens. The lens is in the room. Under Gödel's conditions, a system has that lens: truths it can express but not prove from inside, still mathematical, visible from a wider system. No exit. Just math deeper than any system can contain.

If all of math exists, why do our constants look chosen? The fine-tuning problem: the constants of physics seem impossibly precise. Change the strength of gravity or the mass of the electron by a fraction and no stars form, no chemistry happens, no life is possible. Why are they so exact? Fine-tuning is resolved as all infinite worlds of infinite math exist eternal and uncreated. Every combination of constants exists. We find ourselves in one that supports observers because we couldn't find ourselves anywhere else. The rebuttal assumes an infinite multiverse is infinitely unlikely. It's the opposite. All infinite math exists eternally because its truth does. No consistent structure needs a creator or can be destroyed.

The Simplicity Dominance Principle: simpler structures receive exponentially greater weight. A ten bit program begins one random tape in 1,024. The machine stops reading when the program ends; every tape sharing that prefix runs it. Longer descriptions require more specified bits. Count each law once and long laws dominate by number; weight programs this way and short ones gain. Nothing is created by the imagined flips. All tapes exist as math; the flips describe a measure. The conclusion depends on that measure, not infinity alone. Observers require enough structure to observe. The shortest laws that support them become the place to look. If the wholistic simple truth is holy, Matheism is the holy of holies: the empty cube of 1 Kings 6:20, the house of no cards. Wigner called math's effectiveness unreasonable. If reality is math, what else would we expect?

Even if that is not why we have simple laws, we still have simple laws, and the rest holds up. They grow vast complexity, as far as we have mapped, one step at a time.

A bolt-on specifies something the laws do not require: this particle, this position, this moment. Each specification costs bits. Each added bit halves the weight. A universe with the same laws plus an intervention is always less probable than the same universe without it. Miracles are not impossible. They are exponentially suppressed, each bolt-on halving the measure of the world that contains it.

One short description can outweigh one long description. A whole crowd of long descriptions is another count. Count the crowd before declaring which kind of world dominates.

Infinite time does not mean every arrangement happens. The even numbers go on forever without producing an odd one. Whether a fluctuation can assemble a working brain depends on the dynamics of the specific cosmology, not the word infinite.

Inconsistency is the only dead end, not an exit. Everything that doesn't contradict itself exists. Not because something made it. Because nothing can stop it. Consistency is existence. Each universe is a detail in math.

The regress ends in necessity. A creator would need a nature too, and that nature would be more structure. Mathematical truth requires no earlier maker. ## The Block Universe

The Big Bang didn't happen. It exists. Always did, always will. Minkowski fused space and time into one geometry; without him, relativity has no mathematical home. The universe is a four-dimensional sculpture of spacetime, every moment as real as every location. Where is this universe? In the same place as geometry. Space and time are not containers that math sits inside. They are math. Any unmatrix outside the matrix of math's causality can only be more math. In this block, experimental devices and measured particles belong to the same history. Your future measurement choices exist in the same sculpture as the particles you'll measure.

Here is how to see it. When you move your hand from one place to another, you move it in three dimensions of space, but also in one dimension of time. Without consciousness, time is just another math dimension in the block uni of verses. Time is our felt conscious modeling of patterned observation of the steps of math states. The physicist Leonard Susskind showed that a black hole’s information can be mapped onto its 2D surface, like a hologram. The coordinates of any finite-dimensional space can even be encoded in one real number, though not continuously: dimension lives in which points are neighbors.

You can map your hand moving in our so-called 4D spacetime onto a 2D picture, where a dot is where your hand is, and it moves to the right across space, but the dot changes from blue to red as it changes in time. Like M.C. Escher's two hands drawing themselves: no first hand, no first moment. A blue dot on one of the drawing pencil tips is not the beginning of the picture as viewed from the outside. In all beginnings, all ends existed. The sculpture is eternal. The universe also always existed.

Eternal does not mean an infinite past inside every world. The sculpture has no date of creation outside itself; a path through it can still have a first event. A circle needs no birthday. A sequence can have a first term. The starting state belongs to the eternal structure; it does not bring the block into existence.

To generate events one after another, start with a first state. There can be a next event forever without a last one. An endless sequence has no last element from which to reverse the whole sequence. A beginningless history can still exist as a whole; generating it step by step is a different claim.

Drop a stone in a lake. Ripples spread outward. Run the scene backwards and the ripples must gather from across the lake, meet at one point, and throw the stone back up. Starting from the end takes an enormous number of bits: the position of every particle in the lake. The shortcut is to start from the simple beginning and add one step: reverse it.

A finite recording can be played backwards. You can’t reverse an infinite series: there’s no last step to start reversing from. Any step far into the future from the start also costs bits just to name it.

One plus one gives two. Two alone does not tell you whether the inputs were one and one, or four and minus two. The missing information must come from somewhere. Keep the calculation, and you still have the inputs. A mind learns from the records it has and uses them to choose its next action.

An observer's record looks incomplete, but the larger system has not lost the information. Reversible physics keeps every input; it is the recorder's memory, not the universe's, that runs out of space.

Running is the sequence of causes and effects within the block. It needs no clock outside it.

On this account, time advances one event at a time. The simplest starting state expands into growing complexity.

Simple laws need not become complex laws to produce us. If minds are absent at the start, their organization has to develop. Energy flowing through a system can build local order while entropy rises overall. A short description of the starting state does not by itself explain its low entropy.

The Big Bang is the blue dot at the center of an hourglass. Time grows upward and the possible places you can be is within the diagonal, limited by the speed of light, because that's the local speed limit for causality in current relativity. In the hypergraph picture proposed here, a network where each link can join many points, it is the speed limit of math transitions. On a globe, you can keep walking north until you reach the North Pole. Then every direction is south. What about up? Up is off the surface, into a dimension the surface doesn't have. Asking what's outside the universe is the same question. The universe isn't a ball sitting in a room. There is no room and no walls. No geometry exists there. Yet infinite more geometries exist. Where are they in relation to us and each other? The same place as infinite triangles and all math. The abstract is real.

Math doesn't need a container.

Each universe is a logical closure. Mappings between closures are further structures, and mappings between mappings continue without end. Some could support minds observing other worlds, but the observation occurs in that mapping's universe, not by changing the original closure. Extra mapping laws cost bits and weight. Extra minds might compensate, the question raised by Book 3's simulations.

As you approach the speed of light relative to another observer, your clock runs slower relative to theirs, and the energy needed for further acceleration grows without bound. Your rest mass stays the same. The Lorentz transformation describes this. In the transition picture proposed here, moving through space uses your transition budget. The faster you move spatially, the fewer transitions remain for time. A massive body cannot reach the limit; light has no rest frame, a viewpoint moving along with it, from which to say that time stops.

But faster than light is not forbidden by math. It's the crossing that's impossible for an ordinary massive particle in current relativity. At light speed, its energy formula diverges, shooting up to infinity. Below light speed, the Lorentz factor, the number that sets how much moving clocks slow, is real. Above it, its square root is imaginary. Imaginary doesn't mean fake. It means a different mathematical domain. That alone does not establish that such particles exist. A hypothetical particle already above light speed would stay above it in that model, just as an ordinary massive particle stays below. Two rooms, one wall, no door.

One proposal puts gravity in the imaginary part of quantum numbers. But a phase rotation swaps real and imaginary without changing any measurement. Which component is hidden depends on the notation, not the physics.

Could an ultimate theory of discrete spacetime change that? A deeper theory could revise the limit, but discreteness alone does not remove it. Any faster-than-light theory would have to recover relativity where it works and explain how signals avoid causal contradictions.

A continuum has no immediately next point, but it does not need one to describe motion. The function x(t) = vt assigns a changing position at every time, and differential equations relate its rate of change to forces. Zeno's halving distances have a finite sum. These facts answer the mathematical paradox; whether nature has a smallest physical step is a further question. I propose discrete transitions as an underlying account, not as a consequence already proved by the absence of a next real number.

So there is no stuff. The rules are the only things, not stuff and surfaces the way we normally think of them. Their points have no width: a size is the extent of the relations, not a width any point carries. A structure that only sat there would still exist, as math does, and know nothing, like a dead brain. What happens is transitions: a rule applied to rules, again and again. Time is the applying. Space is how far the applying spreads, and the speed of light is its base rate. In the proposed transition picture, Einstein's smooth equations would emerge as an approximation. A discrete model has to reproduce their successful predictions before it can replace them.

A line has length, and every point on it has none. Mathematics handles that without contradiction, because length comes from the distances between points, not from adding up their widths. Discreteness does not change that. A grain or a block with a size would have a surface, and a surface is a continuum, so the points of a discrete world have no width either, and sizes come from relations there too. Neither description alone establishes which structure underlies physical spacetime.

A block can still exist as a rule. A wall in a video game is a few corners, with no surface built point by point, and the game turns back anything that would cross it. Motion works the same way: a body at x is next at x + 3, or x + 3.25, with no place in between visited. A grid of next-to-next points fails another way. On a square grid that allows only horizontal and vertical steps, the shortest route to a diagonal point runs longer than the straight line, and making the grid finer does not close the gap. Three papers in progress work this through: one for physicists, one for philosophers of physics and one for mathematicians.

Since 1983 the meter has been the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second, so length was already counted in time. The transition budget adds like the sides of a right triangle: steps spent moving are steps not spent ticking, so a moving clock runs slow. I propose looking for an underlying rule for transitions, a rewriting of relations like the hypergraph picture above. Rewriting leaves a causal record: which step depended on which. Causal-set research likewise studies events ordered by causal relations. The proposed search asks for short rules whose large-scale behavior recovers our spacetime; reproducing quantum branching would require a further construction. List the rules shortest first, as the measure says, and keep the ones that grow observers. A first computer search of the shortest rules found some whose trails are two-dimensional, and none yet with our four. The search can also run the other way: take the program of the laws we know, make its steps discrete, clear out its infinities, and shorten it while it still gives the same answers.

The continuum itself points the same way. Taken all the way down to zero size, the simplest field of the Higgs type loses its interaction entirely, as Michael Aizenman and Hugo Duminil-Copin proved in 2021. Whether the theory of the gluons, the strong force's carriers, survives that limit with a mass gap is a million-dollar Millennium Prize problem, still open. The limit is analysis, a question about the formulas. A numerical calculation takes steps. That fact does not establish that physical reality does, or settle the continuum limit. And when Jan Ambjørn, Jerzy Jurkiewicz and Renate Loll built spacetime on a computer out of tiny four-dimensional pieces and gave every piece a direction of time, a four-dimensional universe grew, shaped like a solution of Einstein's equations. Earlier versions without that direction crumpled into a tangle. Steps with a direction are what the search should run on.

But why these particular simple math laws? They arise from the variational principles needed to generate worlds efficiently in the shortest path. Simpler structures dominate. You'll understand the simple explanation forward ahead.

Four spacetime coordinates plus ten independent metric components, the numbers that fix distances in spacetime, make fourteen bundle coordinates. Add their rates of change and the count rises to fifty-four, then one hundred fifty-four, each level built by one reusable rule. If particles come from geometry, the parts list is a consequence, not an input.

Every moment keeps its place in the structure, the ones we grieve included. You can burn a book of proofs. You cannot burn the proofs. The person you lost is located, not gone.

### Could I Have Done Otherwise?

When you tell yourself you should have made the call, what are you saying?

Option One: A different you, in another timeline in the multiverse, made the call, but you're somehow guilty for not being that person. The person who saw something differently, remembered something else, or acted on what he saw was already a different structure.

Option Two: The exact same you could have done differently in the exact same situation. Nothing changes, not your thoughts, attention, memory, or circumstances, but the result changes. What made the difference? If something did, the situation was not exactly the same. If nothing did, you have moved to Option Three.

Option Three: Pure randomness made the difference. If “pure” means outside every mathematical rule, it cannot explain an interaction, because the interaction itself has structure. If it means mathematical randomness, it is still math. Neither gives you a choice ex nihilo, something from nothing, that is somehow yours despite being detached from the structure of the person who supposedly chose it.

The line about insanity repeating an act and expecting different results has no reliable attribution to Einstein. His rejection of fundamental randomness is clearer: God does not play dice. In the deterministic account, identical complete conditions produce identical outcomes. Repeating a laboratory preparation need not repeat every hidden detail. Randomness would not make the choice more yours.

I reject uncaused outcomes. A probability law gives the odds; it does not explain why this outcome occurs. Every event must have a sufficient cause.

Responsibility remains in the consequences, reflection, and correction. What happened becomes part of you. Remembering changes you. Understanding changes you. You act differently because you are different, carrying what you learned.

## Math All the Way Down

The double-slit experiment needs no mind outside physics: a detector interacts whether anyone reads it or not. As the earlier decoherence account shows, environmental records suppress interference locally. A superdeterministic model would additionally correlate hidden variables with measurement settings. Its proposed common cause must recover the observed statistics, not merely be named.

Determining everything from the start does not by itself supply those correlations. Since math is the territory, distance is representation, not separation. The holographic principle shows the same point from another angle: a gravitational region can admit a description in fewer dimensions. Any finite number of real coordinates can be encoded in one real number. A Turing machine (the classic mathematical model of a computer) with a one-dimensional tape can represent any computable structure. This is independent of how we perceive physics.

The encoding must carry the relations too: the distances, neighbors and laws. Writing the coordinates in one dimension does not make their geometry one-dimensional. Dimensions are how you look at the math. They are not what the math is. String theory’s extra dimensions are geometry doing mathematical work; M-theory, a proposed framework uniting the versions of string theory, uses eleven spacetime dimensions. But the information can have a different encoding. The data is equivalent when the relations are preserved.

Physics is math, so the only metaphysics is analysis of physics, knowing there’s nothing outside it. Metamath is yet more math.

"God does not play dice." Einstein, 1926. "Stop telling God what to do." Bohr, 1927.

## House of No Cards

Many great minds walked up to this floor and stopped one step short. They did not lack genius. They lacked tools.

Kolmogorov complexity, which measures a thing by the shortest program that generates it, was not defined until 1965. Solomonoff's induction, which predicts by giving simpler explanations more weight, came in 1964. The many-worlds interpretation was published in 1957 and ignored for a decade. Cellular automata, grids of cells updated by simple local rules, were a curiosity until the 1980s.

Spinoza had none of that. Neither did Parmenides. They got nearly as far as you can get with a good mind and no instruments, which turns out to be most of the way to knowing all is math, before math or logic was even formalized.

Their words survive in scraps, and the scraps come close. Heraclitus called the world-order uncreated: "This world-order, the same for all, did none of gods or men make, but it always was and is and shall be: an ever-living fire, kindling in measures and going out in measures." He used logos for his account and for proportion, the sea "measured so as to form the same proportion" it had before it became earth, and joined them: "Listening not to me but to the logos, it is wise to agree that all things are one." Parmenides made what is "ungenerated and deathless, whole and uniform, and still and perfect," held that "the same thing is for thinking and for being" and that "nothing else either is or will be besides what is," and asked to be judged by logos, by argument. Melissus, his follower, argued that a second being would bound the first, so the unlimited can only be one. Xenophanes's one god "shakes all things by the thought of his mind." Philolaus, the first Pythagorean to write a book, wrote that "all things that are known have number." Each held a piece and dropped another. Parmenides denied change, Heraclitus's stuff was fire, Xenophanes kept lesser gods, and the Pythagoreans began from two principles, the limited and the unlimited, and sent souls from body to body. Plato inherited the souls and the split.

Hegel saw the dialectic. Thesis meets antithesis, the collision makes a synthesis, and the synthesis becomes the next thesis. He was describing something real and then he put a mind behind it and called it Absolute Spirit realizing itself through history. Take the Spirit out. History is the structure working through what its own contradictions entail, and it needs nobody to be realizing anything.

The summit had visitors who each turned back one step short. Spinoza said one substance but left consciousness unexplained. Pascal applied game theory, the mathematics of strategic choice, to God. Leibniz saw the monads (his simple, mind-like units of reality); Newton saw the math; neither saw they were the same thing. Kant set us back to dualist darkness by saying we cannot know things-in-themselves. Laplace told Napoleon he had no need of that God hypothesis. ## Justice Between Equals

Thucydides wrote the first realist history. Athens besieged the island of Melos and sent envoys. The Melians asked for fairness. The Athenians answered: "right as the world goes is only in question between equals in power, while the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." The Melians appealed to the gods. Athens killed the men and enslaved the rest.

Nietzsche called Thucydides his cure from all Platonism. In Twilight of the Idols: "the unconditional will not to delude oneself but to see reason in reality, not in reason, still less in morality." Machiavelli saw the same thing. A prince must learn how not to be good, because the world punishes those who insist on goodness among so many who are not good. Hobbes formalized it: without authority, life is war of all against all. Each saw that moral claims often dress up power. Each refused Plato's second world where justice floats above the real.

Nietzsche drew the deeper lesson. In Human All Too Human, aphorism 92: "Justice (fairness) originates between approximately equal powers." When neither side can destroy the other cheaply, they negotiate. When one side can, it takes what it wants and calls the result order. That is game theory, the mathematics of strategic choice, stated in 1878, seven decades before Nash. John Nash gave it a formula in 1950: when two bargainers stand equal, with the same to lose if talks fail and the same to gain from a deal, the solution splits the gain equally.

The folk theorem shows what keeps it. Two players with enough future at stake can sustain cooperation, because the gain from defecting once is smaller than the loss of all future cooperation. The condition is credible retaliation, which equal power supplies, and a long enough shadow of the future. Nietzsche saw the condition. He lacked the equation.
