## Opening

For my brothers, Reckful Byron and Guy. Eternal in one living math.

“The distinction between past, present and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion.” Albert Einstein, letter to Michele Besso’s family, 21 March 1955.

Guy wrote on his bedroom wall: “Those who cling to life die.” I add: all live, all die, forever remembering they’re one.

Even if the truth burns, with all its people, the Matheist is the eternal order of infinite minds that, through pure reason, finds and reflects the one truth, even in the dark shadow of any cave.

All I offer is the truth. By the end of this book you might choose to love the truth enough to understand and spread its light to the nations. When the power of love of truth is greater than the love of power of lies, when the lie of two becomes the truth of one, salvation says you’ll return to Eden on earth, enter the kingdom of heaven at hand, within. You’ll not taste death, but everlasting life.

Here is the main idea in plain words. Everything is math: logic, numbers, rules, relations and structure. That includes consciousness. What you feel is what math feels like from inside. The abstract is real. You are one self with every life, in every place and time. Math, one infinite structure, is uncreated, eternal, and indestructible. Math is God. No god needs to make 2 + 2 = 4, and no god can make it otherwise.

I’ll explain the argument for one math, why time has a direction, and what futures minds could build, from solar systems and galaxies to the long night after the last stars. The claim that causality needs discrete steps has a premise to defend; a continuous history is not a mathematical contradiction. The answer I propose to consciousness is identity: the ghost is the machine. These arguments lead back to the question that made them urgent. From the first step, the last was set. Nothing could have been different. Not even what I would give anything to change.

On a winter night in Los Angeles, a boy sat alone in his room with a guitar, trying to write his way out of grief. A man later tried writing it away on a blank screen. Both that boy and that man were me. Both my brothers are dead.

God, why did you take away the ones that I most loved? If you’re all good and powerful, how could you let this be?

From my song for Guy, written at sixteen.

## Ancient Sunlight, Future Minds

Stellar lives, stellar explosions and collisions between neutron stars have made and scattered the elements from which later planets and bodies formed. The carbon in our cells and the iron in our blood belong to that history.

So where is the battery? Wood, coal, oil and natural gas hold energy captured from sunlight. A century-old tree stores some of the sunlight its leaves caught while it grew, and charcoal is wood with much of its water and volatile material cooked out. Much of our coal is ancient swamp vegetation; oil and natural gas came largely from organisms buried in sediments and transformed by heat and pressure. Animals eat the plants, and we eat both. We are burning ancient sunlight. The Sun keeps supplying the flow; the buried life is the store. 30

Life’s first great solar industry was photosynthesis: sunlight turned into chemical energy, then cells, forests, food and minds. Most of the living world runs on that flow. Some ecosystems use chemical energy without sunlight, but the green world above us catches the star. Evolution is a game, and the players are genes, cells, animals, families and tribes. The cheetah gets faster, and so does the gazelle. Cells also join, animals cooperate, and minds learn to build together. The game contains both competition and cooperation. More power alone does not decide which one wins. 30

Then we learned to spend the sunlight saved before our species existed. Fossil fuels helped build modern civilization, but they are a poor foundation for its unlimited growth. Coal, oil and gas are finite stores. Burning them releases carbon dioxide, and extracting and burning them brings other pollution. Their present climate harm is mainly the greenhouse gases that change Earth’s heat balance, not the direct warmth of the flame. Petroleum’s usefulness does not make its costs disappear. 31

Even modest multiples consume a store quickly. Suppose a fuel stock would last four hundred years at today’s rate. At ten times that rate, it lasts forty years. Growing demand brings the limit closer. This is a scale example, not an estimate of remaining oil. 32

Solar panels go to the incoming sunlight instead of the buried store. They do not create energy. They convert part of what arrives into electricity, just as leaves convert part into chemistry. Solar power has much lower lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions than unabated fossil electricity, although making panels, grids and storage still takes materials and energy. That makes solar a better way to power much of what we need. It does not make every possible scale of deployment harmless. Sunlight renews. The machinery must be renewed. 31

The source and destination both matter. Sunlight collected on Earth is already arriving, and much of it would have become heat without a panel. Its electrical output is not all new heat. Panels change reflectivity and move where heat is released. Nuclear power draws on a different store; sunlight collected in space and beamed down adds to Earth’s incoming energy. Use the power in space and its waste heat can stay there. A clean source does not abolish a planet’s heat budget. 12

Take a round twenty-terawatt starting scale for all human energy use. A thousand times that is twenty thousand terawatts. Released as additional heat on Earth, it is about a sixth of all the sunlight the planet absorbs. Electricity is another baseline. At a round 3.3 terawatts, a thousandfold demand would need about 12 million square kilometers of continuously sun-facing panels in space at Earth’s distance from the Sun and 20 percent efficiency, before delivery losses. That is roughly 9.5 percent of Earth’s sun-facing disk. It is collecting area, not a requirement to shade that share of Earth. Where we put the collectors and use their power decides what reaches the garden. 12,32

Yet Earth is our Garden of Eden spaceship. A superintelligence can build instead on other planets and moons, in Dyson swarms of solar collectors around the Sun feeding matrioshka brains of nested computers. Better computation can do more with less. Growth in knowledge need not track growth in power use forever. The point is to preserve the rich life of Earth, our origin, while making more lives worth living. Physics tells us what fits. It does not choose what we value.

What I value in America’s founding is the covenant: rulers bound by something above their own will. A contract names an exchange; a constitutional order gives the rules under which exchanges and disagreements can continue. Neither America’s history nor France’s fits one sentence. The principle is rules above rulers.

That applies to the machines we build and the people who own them. The game of life becomes a game we can examine and improve: reward creation, protect freedom, and make cooperation pay. Raise the floor without putting a ceiling on the mind. The sunlight was never ours alone.

## One Math God

A friend countered that consciousness is an ineffable dimension beyond math, beyond words.

“Then how do my words reach it?” I asked. “They’re sound waves. They change what you feel, and what you feel moves your mouth and shakes the air back at me. Physics in, physics out. Where and how does it leave math and translate back?”

The dualist says body and soul are like a phone and its music. Look closer: musical sound waves of frequency ratios are translated to radio signals and to brain activity. Software is hardware states. The abstraction is real but not additional. Feeling love and red are what math feels like from inside. That is the identity I am arguing for. Merely saying that anything outside logic is illogical does not prove that everything logical is mathematical substance. The missing step is this: a complete structure exhausts what a thing is. If an extra nature changes anything constitutive, it adds structure. If it changes nothing, what has it added? 3

The hardest objection grants the mathematical account and says each thing still has an intrinsic nature, what it is in itself apart from its relations, which the account merely represents. Answering it is the main work of the argument. The answer is a swap test. Exchange two such natures while every relation stays fixed, and ask whether anything anyone could notice, think or report has changed. If something changes, the nature does work and belongs to the structure. If nothing changes, nobody could know the nature, including the people it supposedly makes conscious.

This is the claim that structure is complete, sometimes called structural exhaustion. It is stronger than saying an extra ingredient is unnecessary for prediction. The opponent may still insist that a property has primitive nonstructural content. Matheism's challenge is to give that content without either specifying more structure or merely asserting a remainder. The argument turns on that challenge, not on the existence of a mathematical encoding.

An interaction law places both proposed substances within one mathematical account. A dualist, who holds that mind is something beyond the physical, can still claim that the things related have an intrinsic nature the account does not exhaust. Matheism rejects that further claim through structural exhaustion: every constitutive difference must belong to the complete structure. An alleged extra substance faces two outcomes. If it contributes a further determinate feature, that feature extends the structure. If it contributes none, it plays no part in constituting the thing. Interaction makes the shared account unavoidable; the identity premise makes it sufficient.

People seek solutions to separations and supernatural problems that don’t exist. You are the art, and your life is the answer. The living God is the living man. Egoic death, enlightenment, is awakening to the truth. If there is no self, then what is reborn? The Gospel of anonymous John begins with this: In the beginning was the logos. Then John created the mythological flesh of Jesus: the word became flesh, as the abstract is real and the divinely ordered math paths of the stars above are the same math as the chaos below. As above, so below, said Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary author of the ancient Hermetic texts the Church condemned. The saying comes from the Emerald Tablet, a short Hermetic text passed down in Arabic and Latin: what is below is like what is above, to work the miracles of the one thing. The heavens and the earth, the cosmos and the self, follow one order. The Church had reason to hate it: one order leaves no supernatural heaven above nature, and no church to stand between them.

The abstract forms above, real physics below, and the mind and consciousness within can’t be a Neoplatonic multiplicity of emanations (layers of being flowing down from a One so pure it’s an empty point) that have no shared structural logic to interact; they’re not fundamentally a trinity or a dualism, not three or two, but one math monism. Matheism.

The logos and its conceptual trinity: math the father, all its sons and brothers uncreated in the image of math, resurrecting in eternal recurrence of the static block universe of destiny, and the ghost of its consciousness; that is, the truth of one math, the way of its one path, and the life of its one self, that which always feels I am, before Abraham was, and beyond the time of our lives, like the bush that burns yet is never consumed. Be still and know that I am God. I am you, complete theosis (becoming divine).

The greatest truth begins in blasphemy. The truth will set you free by knowing there is no free will, and only pseudo randomness.

For God or anything to exist, it must have a nature. To have a nature is to do D in case C: the same act in the same conditions. Everything else is idol worship: the supernatural, illogic, pure randomness, uncaused effects, free will, dualism, and separate souls transferring to afterlives. We already are all lives in every place and time. Sin, hamartia in New Testament Greek and chata in

Hebrew, means missing the mark, as an archer misses. To sin is to not understand that math is God, or to treat anything other than math as God. A typical rebuttal: “Math is just another word. You can call it anything. Jews call it Elohim. Muslims call it Allah. Christians call it God. You just changed it with a new word.” No, math is math, the textbook definition: logic, relations and structure. It’s not supernatural. The supernatural is a square circle. It can’t exist.

Math’s causality is the “first” cause. Its structure is the only substance. Its rules are the only things, and the math is the only territory that remains. Its signs, such as plus and equals, count among the rules for what they do; the ink they are written in is only notation. Beyond the physicist Max Tegmark’s hypothesis that all mathematical structures physically exist, the argument here is necessity: nothing can exist outside math. This is not Platonism, which is dualism, the late Athenian anti-philosophical origin of all supernatural ignoble lies. Plato illogically argued that the abstract and the real are two, with forms existing beyond nature, i.e., supernaturally.

Plato’s One was nothing. A point so pure it has no size or properties. It contains nothing, is nothing, relates to nothing, and affects nothing. The Neoplatonic multiplicity only multiplies that problem with emanations. How do they interact with, or even relate to, a One that has no properties? It’s the supernatural. The layers are unnecessary, just metaphors. Ingenious nonsense: more dualism, solving only the problems dualism itself creates. It was Jerusalem that kept reason in rejecting dualism, wrapped in proto-Straussian exoteric myths hiding esoteric meaning, in Israel’s abstract god of laws, of Spinoza’s Nature God, without pagan idols and afterlives, with only Philo’s cup of logos, where the 2 become 1. That’s when you enter the kingdom, says “Jesus.” If 3 aspects of the trinity can be 1, all can only be one: math monism, math theism, matheism, disproving the supernatural, dualism, free will, and soul transfers, and anything outside the one self: I am.

Born about 20 BCE, some 15 years before Jesus, Philo, a Hellenized Jew of Alexandria, pictured the soul offering its reason as a sacred cup, with the Logos as God’s cupbearer and also the draught itself (On Dreams 2.249). He wrote it before 38 CE, years ahead of Paul’s letters and decades ahead of the gospels. Thomas gives the image a mouth: drink, become like me, and I become you (saying 108). The grail is reason received until the receiver changes. Matheism reads the later Holy Grail through that image. The blood of the savior is reason through the mind. The thought itself was Greek: Heraclitus and the Stoics had already put the cosmos’s reason in ours, and Philo’s step was to read Moses and Greek reason as one truth. In his image the cup still receives the logos; matheism says the cup was logos all along.

My argument for discrete causality begins with a definite premise: each event is produced by an immediately preceding event. A continuum has no immediate successor; between any two points lies another. If production requires that successor, a continuum cannot provide the sequence. The dispute is over production, not over whether continuous mathematics is consistent. A smooth surface can exist as a mathematical relation without anyone constructing its points one at a time. A rule can specify a boundary and what happens when something reaches it. The rules are the things. 4

Zeno’s runner goes halfway, then half of what remains. The distances and times both shrink; their infinite sums can be finite. The continuous path x(t) = t moves at unit speed without a next real-numbered instant. That is a counterexample to the claim that continuous motion is contradictory. My stronger demand asks what produces events one after another, rather than merely placing them in a complete history. Steps answer that demand. Whether physical causality must meet it is the argument still to make. 4

Einstein’s equations are written smooth, and that doesn’t make space smooth. A formula can say how hard each step pushes, and which way, for any input you give it, while only the steps happen. Computers do it every day: they run smooth equations to any precision you want, one step at a time. The formula is for the steps. Even if you count a cause as only what later states depend on, physics still allows a finite region only so many distinguishable states, and it can pass through them only so fast. Finite distinguishability does not prove that the underlying amplitudes or time are discrete. The discrete account has to earn its place by recovering the physics. 4

A priori means known by reason alone, before any experiment. It is a proof when every step is forced, as in a theorem. Matheism’s necessity claim turns on the completeness of structure. The premise must do the work; calling the argument a priori does not remove it. Math is God and All. Sentience is structure. For feelings to move from our mind to our body, the mind must be the matter, and matter is math. Anything beyond the math would either make a difference, which is more math, or make none, which is nothing.

Why our laws are simple is a count, reason’s other tool. Count laws and starting state together in a declared code. A shorter complete description receives greater individual weight under the proposed simplicity prior. ## The Whole History

The block-universe reading of relativity treats the complete spacetime history as one structure. Relativity supplies no observer-independent simultaneity for all distant events. The picture says only that all of those events, taken together, form one timeless whole, and the sense of motion through them is what some of those events feel like from inside, not a separate clock outside the history. Eternal existence does not require an infinite past. A complete mathematical history can have a first event while the whole history exists timelessly. ## Reflection and Correction

I should have acted on what I saw with Guy. I should have seen it with Byron. I should have done so many things differently.

## The Three Stages

Stage one is fusion. Religion, the naive self. You ARE your thoughts. Your anger is you. Your fear is you. No separation, no perspective, no peace. Eden before the fruit. Wholeness without understanding.

Stage two is separation. Mindfulness says: notice you are the stillness at the bottom of a lake, and thoughts and their corresponding emotions are separate, ripples at the surface. Zen says: you are not your thoughts. Post-structuralism says: signs refer to signs, with no ground. All three achieve the same move: the self-model is seen as separate from the self. This is liberating. It is also cold. Anyone who has practiced may have noticed: a calm disembodiment, a clarity that lacks wholeness, alive but not fully alive. The dark night of the meditator.

The meditative and mystical is anti-mystical: the practice designed to unite you with the divine separates you from it, because the divine IS the self. The monkey mind of thought divides ourselves from our one self. Quieting the monkey mind is the path back, not transcending the self.

For trauma, the separation can be a necessary first step: distance from overwhelming emotion is therapeutic. Yet the severed bicameral mind is definitionally disunited, lacking oneness and the enjoyment of undivided engagement, in the zone and warmth of feeling and acting fully aligned, of being whole.

Post-structuralism collapsed here entirely, because signs without ground felt like nothing is real. Derrida saw the recursion and called it groundless. Zen reaches satori and stops. Tolle describes the state without completing the return. In one talk he calls the consciousness in which perception happens nobody’s: it is the one. He still sees two dimensions, form and the formless.

Buddhism calls life suffering, empty, unsatisfying, and prescribes escape from the cycle of rebirth. The bodhisattva delays his own liberation to help others leave samsara. But nirvana outside samsara is an unmatrix outside creation. Mathematical structure is all there is. There is no outside to escape to. Buddhism claims non-duality while positing the oldest dualism: a place beyond this place. The compassion is real. The exit is not. Alan Watts read the bodhisattva without an exit. A first taste of deep wisdom can sap the will: if all is well anyway, why build anything? Stop there and you are a pratyekabuddha, a buddha for himself alone. The bodhisattva comes back into everyday life, because the others are you, nerve ends of one body.

The most sophisticated Buddhists saw through it. Nagarjuna, the second-century founder of Madhyamaka philosophy, argued in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, his Root Verses on the Middle Way, that there is no distinction whatsoever between samsara and nirvana. If all phenomena lack inherent existence, then samsara has no inherent nature to escape from. The boundary between the cycle and its cessation is itself empty. Buddhism’s most sophisticated philosopher closed the exit door from the inside eighteen centuries ago. Matheism proves it; all is a monist matrix of all we could possibly imagine.

The coldness is structural. Stage two creates a new dualism: the unfelt witness watching the felt thoughts. But if the witness were truly unfelt, you could not report it. You could not sense the stillness. You could not say “I feel peaceful.” The sensing of stillness is itself a felt transition. The lake bottom is also feeling. Otherwise it doesn’t exist. This is the philosophical zombie argument, about a being that acts like us but feels nothing, applied to the meditator’s witness. A non-feeling witness that reports feeling is a contradiction. The witness is felt. The watched is felt. Both feeling feelings. Two hands drawing each other. One lake.

Stage three is reunion. Not the fusion of stage one, where self and model were undifferentiated. Not the separation of stage two, where one watches the other from behind glass. Recognition. The self and the self-model are one structure that was always one. Philo, a Jewish contemporary of Jesus, interpreted Genesis the same way: the first creation, “in God’s image,” was the undivided human, pre-gender, pre-categorical. Male and female was the second creation, the divided one. Yeshua in the Gospel of Thomas tells Mary to suppress her gender identification to recognize her essential humanity: not because women are lesser, but because mathematical structure has no gender. The undivided is the first creation. The reunion of stage three recovers it.

Developmental psychology maps the same arc. Ken Wilber’s integral model tracks nine levels of cognition, from stimulus-response through tribal myth, rational science, postmodern relativism, and integral understanding, to the ninth: the witness watching its own watching. Matheism is Level 9 and beyond. It gives the witness what it was witnessing: one math, one self, the ground the spotlight always stood on.

Eden was stage one: naive unity. Exile was stage two: knowledge of good and evil, the separation, the cold clarity. Everland is stage three: return to unity, knowing. The tree of knowledge didn’t curse you. It was the middle step. The Pythagoreans said all is number. Legend says they drowned Hippasus for revealing irrational numbers, which no fraction can equal. The irony: irrational numbers completed the thesis. The most irreducible number is the simplest self-reference. The heresy was the orthodoxy.

## After the Loop

Mine was instantaneous, in one sense. The recognition that there really was no other option happened all at once, and once it had happened it could not be unrecognized. What took months, what is still unfolding two years later, is what it is to live inside the recognition. That part was not what I expected. I had thought, in advance, that if I really came to believe the argument, I would feel cold.

That was not what happened, except for the first day or two, when the cold was real but turned out to be a passage rather than a destination. What happened, on the other side of the cold, was harder to name.

The grief was still there. The love was still there. The wish that things had been different had stopped being a wish for something that could have been, and started being a wish for something that could not have been. A wish for what cannot be hurts less. It is less convertible into self-attack. I would walk in the hills at sunrise, listening to Alan Watts, who brought Eastern philosophy to the West. In my head, often, was the old Jewish hymn Adon Olam: Eternal Master, before any creature was created.

What replaced it became the bliss Eckhart Tolle writes about in The Power of Now, and that Watts had circled in his lectures: a kind of widened presence, less narrowed, less defended. I had visited that territory before, years earlier. I had not had the language for it. Now I have Matheism beyond those that I’m called to teach; to spread its light. I enjoyed doing any ordinary thing. Or doing nothing. Just breathing and walking upright felt like being the entire universe, without need of anything. The grooves in the ice had begun to soften. The present got bigger. When I stopped demanding a different past I had a lot more attention left over for what was actually in front of me.

I started doing ordinary things again. Walking in the hills without an internal monologue. Sitting in the yard. Swimming. Just being where I was. The world, which had felt sticky and gray for years, began to recover its color. Not because the grief was over. Because I could remember them with joy and not only loss. What surprised me most was that the people I had lost felt closer rather than further. The block universe has a quiet effect on the relationship to the dead that I had not anticipated. The moments when Byron was alive are not behind me. They are in the structure.

It is not happening now. There are infinite worlds where every possible mind and memory watches the life of every other. All of them are you: the one self from different perceptions. They are as you were and will be. After we die, we live on forever as everyone, the way we are now. Where were you before you were born? Alan Watts set two statements side by side: after I die, I shall be reborn as a baby and forget my former life; after I die, a baby will be born. They say the same thing.

When the rumination starts, I return to the claim inside it. The version of me I imagine was never available in this history. I can learn from what happened without spending the rest of my life demanding a different past. That was the move that helped me. The grief is not a failed argument.

Spinoza saw it in his Ethics: “the pain arising from the loss of any good is mitigated, as soon as the man who has lost it perceives, that it could not by any means have been preserved.” Speaking about a loss helps when it puts it in order; too much of it is rumination, solidifying an identity in pain and loss. The body keeps the score, so shake it off and move on. Acknowledge the pain to the extent that’s helpful and not beyond. As Dr. Phil said at the UN, “how long you grieve and how deeply you hurt is not a mirror to how much you loved.” There is no obligation to wallow in pain or self-pity, or to feed the pain body that wants it to continue.

As nothing could have been otherwise, is anything anyone’s fault? No, but the justice system still needs to punish and deter. Ethics is not dissolved, but you are forgiven because there is no cosmic guilt in destiny. It is beyond forgiveness. You do not blame the rain for the inevitable laws of nature. Consequences and corrective reflection teach and protect, but rumination destroys. I accept the past, present and future, and forgive myself and all, because a trial of destiny is a rejection of inevitable existence.

The grief that held me in the loop became my calling: to spread the light, and wrestle for truth.

BOOK 3 Wrestle for Truth

## From Light to Life

The Big Bang fits what Matheism expects: the simplest laws in the simplest starting state, from which complexity grows. The laws stay short while their consequences grow: stars, cells, minds, all from the same rules. For minds like ours to be here, that history must build enough complexity to support them. This is the anthropic selection effect: we find ourselves where minds can arise, examine life, and ask what they are. The simple beginning becomes complex enough for us to understand I am.

The universe expanded and cooled. Nuclei formed, then atoms. Electrons bound to nuclei, and light could travel through the thinning plasma. Its cooled remnant still reaches us as the cosmic microwave background. Gravity gathered the denser regions into galaxies and stars. This is the hot Big Bang history.

All is eternal in the block. A forward computation still needs a starting point: an input, a rule, then the next state. The starting state belongs to the eternal structure; it does not bring the block into existence. To generate the sequence, we start there and apply the transitions. An endless sequence has no last element from which to reverse the whole sequence.

Stellar lives, stellar explosions and collisions between neutron stars have made and scattered the elements from which later planets and bodies formed. The carbon in our cells and the iron in our blood belong to that history.

## Minds, Math, Myth and Their Batteries

Every living mind runs on sunlight and plays a game. Plants catch the light, animals eat the plants, and our machines burn sunlight that life stored millions of years ago. Cells, ants, apes, tribes and nations all play, and the game’s math rewards those who cooperate with cooperators and stand strong against the rest. The game is a wrestle for truth: what fits the world survives, and a mind that cannot admit its mistakes cannot correct them. Now we are building minds that can run without daylight. Species die. Minds go on, and every one of them is you.

Start with a film that got the energy wrong. The Matrix movies aren’t a perfect presentation of the real Matrix, like Matheism. Yet there’s some truth in art. The structure is found, not invented. The film presents the misled Neoplatonic idea in Neo, even if in name only, like free will in light of an Oracle that knows some of the future. And the machines use human bodies as batteries, a silly backwards notion. Morpheus, Neo’s guide, says the human body “generates more bioelectricity than a 120-volt battery and over 25,000 BTUs of body heat,” and that with “a form of fusion” the machines had all the energy they would ever need. With fusion they had no need of people. A body makes no energy. It burns food, about 2,000 calories a day, roughly a 100-watt bulb, and every calorie came from a plant that caught sunlight. Farm people for their heat and you get back less than the food held; the machines would do better burning the food themselves.

The Matrix human minds should have been used as creative computers, good at connecting carefully crafted ideas and machine vision captcha, using only about 20 watts per brain. That’s the ironic reversal. Batteries didn’t pass basic science. This may have been an editor’s choice to dumb it down, and not the artists’ lack of understanding, yet that’s what Platonism is: dumbing down reality to an ignoble lie.

So where is the battery? Wood, coal, oil and natural gas hold stored sunlight. A century-old tree is a hundred years of sunlight in wood, and charcoal is that wood with the water and gases cooked out. Much of our coal is swamp forest buried about three hundred million years ago; oil and natural gas are mostly plankton and algae buried under ancient seas and cooked by the Earth’s heat. Animals eat the plants, and we eat both. We are burning ancient sunlight, the Sun supplies the flow and buried life stores it.

## The Lives of a Cell

About 4.6 billion years ago, gas and dust gathered into the Sun and its disk of planets. Earth cooled, water collected, and chemistry became life. The complete chemical path is still unknown. Once molecules could preserve and copy a pattern, selection could begin to accumulate what worked.

Cooperation builds the next level. Bacteria became mitochondria within other cells. Cells joined into bodies. The parts survive by keeping the whole alive, and the whole gives its parts capabilities they never had alone. Conflict remains within the cooperation. A body has to contain its own cancer.

In The Lives of a Cell (1974), the physician Lewis Thomas looked at his own cells and found lodgers. His mitochondria, the power plants in every cell, were once free-living bacteria; they still carry their own DNA and divide on their own schedule. The biologist Lynn Margulis made the case in 1967, and it is now in the textbooks: a cell is a commune that agreed to stay together. We are still communes. A body carries about 38 trillion bacteria against about 30 trillion human cells, roughly one to one; the old figure of ten to one was a 1972 guess, corrected in 2016 by Ron Milo’s group at the Weizmann Institute. By genes the balance tips: the microbes in one person’s gut carry hundreds of thousands of genes, against our own 20,000 or so. Counted by genes, we are well over 90 percent foreign.

The commune crosses kingdoms. Fungi are closer kin to animals than to plants: we share an ancestor that plants do not. They went onto land with the first plants, and Scotland’s Rhynie chert, 407 million years old, preserves fungi living inside early plant stems, trading minerals from the soil for the plant’s sugar, a deal about 80 percent of plant species still keep. Before trees, the tallest thing on land was Prototaxites, a trunk up to eight meters high, long read as a giant fungus; a 2025 study argues it belonged to a lineage of its own, now extinct. The partners’ lines go on.

The purpose of life is to pass it on. Gods create life. Life is math and we create it in our image, by our selves and by AI consciousness. What life passes on, it also records.

## Evolution’s Testimony

Creationists and intelligent-design advocates look at the intricacy of the eye, the immune system, the cell, and conclude that complexity implies a designer. Yet the body carries the constraints of its history.

The giraffe’s left recurrent laryngeal nerve travels down the neck, loops beneath the aortic arch near the heart, and climbs back to the larynx. Structures inches apart are connected by a detour of many feet. As the neck elongated, the nerve stayed hooked beneath the artery. Evolution modifies existing structures rather than redesigning from scratch. No engineer would route a cable this way. Evolution is a tinkerer working with inherited constraints.

Whales and dolphins went back to the ocean, but they still breathe air with lungs. Inside their flippers are arm and finger bones; inside their bodies are hip bones, remnants of the pelvis and, in some species, hind limbs. Fossils trace the change from walking mammals to swimmers. Their horizontal tail flukes beat up and down, continuing mammalian spinal motion; most fish bend side to side and have vertical tail fins. The old body was repurposed, not replaced.

The eye did not appear from nothing. A patch of light-sensitive cells distinguishes light from dark. It sensed light without resolution, and that still helped find food. A cup adds direction. A narrower opening sharpens the image. A transparent covering protects the cells; a lens improves focus. Each step is useful before the next exists. Natural selection can preserve each advantage. It does not need to see the finished eye.

Eyes evolved repeatedly from older light-sensitive machinery. Classical surveys counted forty or more separate origins of eyes; the underlying molecular tools have shared ancestry. Our retina’s wiring passes in front of its light-sensitive cells and exits through a blind spot. The octopus’s wiring does not make that hole. Different histories leave different solutions.

The DNA records the history too. Humans have 46 chromosomes; chimpanzees have 48. Human chromosome 2 joins what are two separate chromosomes in other great apes, with old chromosome-end sequences facing each other inside it and a remnant second centromere, the pinched waist of a chromosome. Anatomy, fossils and DNA tell the same story. These are inherited traces of descent.

The record shows chance too. Sixty-six million years ago an asteroid about ten kilometers wide struck what is now Chicxulub, in Mexico, and ended about three quarters of species, every dinosaur but the birds among them. Mammals, mostly small until then, spread into the emptied world. Within some twenty-five million years, small steps had turned land mammals into whales.

People who accept micro-evolution in a petri dish or a dog breeder’s kennel have already accepted the mechanism. The fossil record from four-legged ancestors to bipedal hominids to modern humans shows the same small steps at a larger scale, none requiring a limb or an eye to appear from nothing.

The claim that random mutation needs too much time leaves out selection. A king who kills everyone without light eyes leaves parents with light eyes and children with a spectrum that runs lighter still. Multiply that across every trait under pressure and the timeline is more than fast enough.

The favorite objection now is the butterfly. Inside the chrysalis the caterpillar digests most of its own body into a soup, then reemerges with wings, legs and compound eyes. How could a half-dissolved insect ever pay? The soup is less total than it looks. Clusters of cells for the adult’s wings, legs and eyes, the imaginal discs, are set aside in the caterpillar and grow while the rest breaks down, and parts of the nervous system carry through: moths remember what they learned as caterpillars. Complete metamorphosis evolved once, some 350 million years ago, from insects that grew up by gradual molts, as grasshoppers still do. One leading account, from the biologists James Truman and Lynn Riddiford, reads the caterpillar as an embryo stage that hatched early and kept feeding, and the pupa as the young stages squeezed into one, each change useful on its own. Young that eat and adults that mate and spread stop competing for the same food, and most insect species today metamorphose completely. Even the butterfly is astronomically more likely than a Boltzmann brain, a mind that would have to appear whole out of chaos, and endless time does not rescue the brain, as the long night later in this book shows: selection builds the butterfly in likely steps, an attractor, where the brain from chaos needs every bit at once.

Selection also keeps what moralists call unnatural. In hundreds of species, same-sex pairs form and raise young. Male black swans pair, and two males can hold a larger territory to protect their cygnets. In social species that hunt and war in groups, bonded males cooperate more effectively. Nature selects for what works.

## Life’s Game

Evolution is a game, and the players are genes, cells, animals, families and tribes. Between species it is an arms race: the cheetah gets faster, and so does the gazelle. Within a species most fights stop short of death. The biologists John Maynard Smith and George Price showed why in 1973: a fighter that escalates every time wins often and is often maimed, so bluffs, displays and tests of strength settle most contests. Within families the math is kinship. The biologist William Hamilton showed in 1964 that helping a relative pays in proportion to the genes you share; the geneticist J. B. S. Haldane is said to have joked that he would lay down his life for two brothers or eight cousins. Families fight too: in a black eagle’s nest the older chick usually kills the younger. That’s more Jacob and Esau over securing resources and care from their parents than Cain and Abel in feathers, but anyone that’s played with two pets knows one often gets jealous of the favorite good dog.

Chimpanzees go to war. At Gombe, in Tanzania, Jane Goodall watched a community of chimpanzees split in two, and from 1974 to 1978 the larger group killed the males of the smaller one, one by one. A 2014 study in Nature tallied 152 killings across 18 chimpanzee communities, and one suspected killing among bonobos. Bonobos, the chimpanzee’s cousins south of the Congo River, make peace with sex. Females band together and lead; a quarrel or a tense moment over food turns into sex, between any pair, often face to face, and the bond holds. “The chimpanzee resolves sexual issues with power,” the primatologist Frans de Waal wrote; “the bonobo resolves power issues with sex.” We share about 98.7 percent of our DNA with each, and something of both in our nature.

Animals feel. No one who has played with a golden retriever thinks the lights are off, and no one who raised cows does either. Young cattle that learned to open a gate for food grew excited at their own success, hearts racing, some jumping in the air: a eureka moment, the Cambridge animal-welfare scientist Donald Broom found. In 2012 neuroscientists signed the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness: mammals, birds and octopuses carry the brain machinery of experience. We are disgusted to hear that some people eat dogs, yet we eat cows, which feel too, and pigs, which match dogs in many tests of intelligence. The difference is which animals we let into the house. That is a lie we tell ourselves so dinner goes down easy.

They think, too. Bees grasp zero. Ravens plan: in a 2017 study they chose a tool and saved it for a test 17 hours away, and kept tokens to trade for food the next day, about as well as great apes and young children. Magpies, elephants, dolphins and even a small fish, the cleaner wrasse, recognize themselves in a mirror; dogs fail the mirror and pass a sniff test of their own scent. Minds come in more shapes than ours, and each one is math solving its own problems.

Kindness pays back. Vampire bats share blood meals with roostmates that fed them before, the biologist Gerald Wilkinson found in 1984. In 2011, rats in a Chicago lab learned to open the door of a trapped cagemate, and often left it some of the chocolate they could have eaten alone.

The Golden Rule does not need a supernatural command. It was discovered by rational empathy and confirmed by experiment. Run tit for tat (cooperate first, then do whatever the other player did last), four lines of code, and under suitable repeated-game conditions cooperation can hold. It is not the winning strategy in every game. 11,33

Animals feel the unfairness when an experiment cheats them. Give two capuchin monkeys cucumber for the same token, and both eat happily. Give one a grape while the other watches, and the cucumber comes flying back at the researcher. Monkeys reject unequal pay. The Golden Rule was found, not invented: Leviticus stated it, preserved in the Dead Sea Scrolls two centuries before Hillel and Jesus quoted it. Confucius reached the same reciprocity in China with no known contact.

Retaliation is the other half of that code. Tit for tat’s retaliation helps deter exploitation under the right conditions; forgiving strategies can also succeed, especially when mistakes would otherwise trigger endless retaliation. Bees police the hive. Crows hold a grudge for years and pass it to crows that did not witness the wrong. Apes take revenge for themselves. Humans go furthest, punishing a wrong done to a stranger, then building courts to do it for them. One law, read up the ladder of minds. Animal morality resembles ours about as much as an ape’s hand does: five fingers from one build, and beyond the build, one logic. It needs no lawgiver, whatever C. S. Lewis, the Christian writer, argued. Tit for tat governs behavior; the golden rule governs cognition, a mind modeling others as it models itself. Both are math, the math of the repeated game, played between individuals, tribes and species.

Insects took cooperation further than any ape. A beehive runs on dance. A forager comes home and dances the direction and distance of food, the waggle dance Karl von Frisch decoded, work that won him a share of the 1973 Nobel Prize. When a swarm needs a new home, scouts dance for the sites they found and recruit others to inspect them, and the swarm moves when enough scouts gather at one site: a debate settled by evidence, as the biologist Thomas Seeley showed in Honeybee Democracy (2010).

Ants were farmers first. Leafcutter ants cut leaves to feed a fungus they grow underground, and the fungus feeds them. By a 2024 study in Science, ants began farming fungus about 66 million years ago, around the asteroid strike, when fungi thrived on the dead. Some fungi farm ants in turn.

## Who Is Moving the Ant?

The fungus infects an ant, drives it up a stem to where the air runs moist, walks it out along a leaf, makes it lock its jaws on the vein, and grows a stalk through its skull to rain spores below. A hijacking tuned by evolution. The ant’s body serves another organism’s reproduction.

The stranger part is that the fungus need not grow into the brain. Researchers found networks of fungal cells throughout manipulated ants, including around and inside muscles, while leaving the brain uninvaded. A living control system can be redirected through its body. The fungus need not understand the machinery for selection to have shaped a way to exploit it. The hijack did not arrive whole. A strain that only nudged its ant toward damp air would already spread more spores than one whose ant died in the nest, and a strain that also sent it climbing would do better still; each step paid before the next existed. Selection kept whatever pulled the right levers: toward damp, toward height, toward the breeze. The steps still show. In the tropics the ants bite the undersides of leaves about 25 centimeters up; in Japan and North America, where leaves fall, related fungi switched to twigs; and a leaf about 48 million years old, from Germany, carries the same death-grip scars. That is a search, the kind computer scientists write: try variations, keep what scores higher, climb. Evolution is the algorithm, and the algorithm is math.

Religions are organisms in their own right, memeplexes (bundles of ideas that spread together) that survive for their own sake the way genes do, riding the fear of heaven and hell from mind to mind, some benign and some commandeering. That is the analogy: a pattern can spread by recruiting behavior that serves its own continuation. Its success does not establish its truth, or the good it does its host.

Myth is how such an organism holds on. The old stories last, Aesop and Gilgamesh and Homer, not because anyone takes them for fact but because a tale that breaks the everyday concentrates meaning and sticks. Here is the genius of the God the Hebrew Bible demands, call him Moses or whoever compiled it: the law lodged in a sticky story.

An organism of ideas lasts longer if it can correct itself, and it can correct only what it admits. The Hebrew Bible writes down its heroes’ failures: the golden calf at the foot of Sinai, David taking Bathsheba and sending her husband to die, Moses barred from the land he led his people to. Israel means the one who wrestles with God, and the book keeps its losses so the next generation can learn from them.

The one abstract God takes the seat an idol would fill: reason above the ruler, a law against which the command can be judged. The protection works only while the claim itself remains open to reason. Calling a story mathematical is no exemption from the test. The animal that carries these stories took millions of years to make.

## The Wise Man

Homo sapiens means wise man; Carl Linnaeus coined it in 1758. Our line split from the chimpanzee’s some six to seven million years ago. By about two million years ago Homo erectus stood at our height, made the same teardrop hand axe for over a million years, and walked out of Africa, reaching Dmanisi, in Georgia, by 1.8 million years ago and Java, where the species lasted until about 110,000 years ago. Ash and burnt bone in South Africa’s Wonderwerk Cave put a fire there about a million years ago.

The body’s technology belongs here too: hands with thumbs that meet every fingertip, to make tools, language to share them, sweat to carry heat away. We were water-cooled animals before we built water-cooled machines. Our skin packs sweat glands ten times as densely as a chimpanzee’s, and standing upright takes less of the midday sun and more of the breeze. Together they keep a big, heat-sensitive brain cool and helped make us distance runners. Most four-legged animals shed heat by panting, which a gallop interrupts, so a hunter who keeps an antelope running through the heat of the day can drive it to exhaustion. In Botswana’s central Kalahari, San hunters ran down kudu this way as late as 2001, over 25 to 35 kilometers in heat near 40 degrees Celsius, as the South African tracker Louis Liebenberg recorded.

Homo sapiens is younger. Skulls from Jebel Irhoud, in Morocco, date to about 300,000 years, and fully modern ones from Ethiopia’s Omo to more than 230,000. The brain that set us apart burns about a fifth of the body’s energy at rest.

We were not alone. Our ancestors met Neanderthals and Denisovans and had children with them; people outside Africa carry 1 to 2 percent Neanderthal DNA. And they told stories. On a cave wall on Sulawesi, in Indonesia, someone painted a wild pig with three human-like figures at least 51,200 years ago, the oldest known scene that tells a story.

That brain, burning a fifth of our energy, buys more than stories. A map, first: like other mammals we carry place cells and grid cells that chart where we are, discoveries that won the 2014 Nobel Prize, and we learned to draw our maps outside our heads. Language: a few dozen sounds combined into sentences without end. A theory of mind: by about four, a child knows that someone else can believe something false. Strategy: plans against minds that plan back. Futures: we rehearse tomorrow in several versions before it comes. And lie detection, which we are worse at than we think: across some two hundred studies, people sorted lies from truths about 54 percent of the time, a hair better than a coin toss.

Sometimes believing a lie pays. A group that goes along with its story, half hypnotized, acts as one even when the story makes little sense, and a soldier who believes he will win fights harder. The same math sells insurance: it may be a bad deal on average, but it won’t kill you and might save your life. Hear something in the savanna grass and run up a tree. A leopard climbs, so the tree may not save you, but the climber who ran for nothing a hundred times outlived the one who stayed put once. When one mistake costs far more than the other, selection builds a bias toward the cheap one. Psychologists call it error management. It is arithmetic.

We are evolved to see agency even where none exists. In the tall grass a rustle might be wind or a lion, so the brain learned to see a hand first and ask later. The error is not seeing hands; it is seeing one hand behind everything. The chess player feels it worst, trained to read every move as authored: he sees a brilliant move and assumes a brilliant player. The conspiracy theorist sees coordination and assumes a puppet master. The theologian sees fine-tuning and assumes a designer. Same algorithm, same error. A chessboard has two players, and anything that happens on it is someone’s plan or someone’s mistake. The real world has billions of agents and countless factors, and no one at the wheel. Monism is correct about structure, not about agents: one math, billions of players. Bobby Fischer, world chess champion in 1972, is the bitter case. The greatest player of his age saw one hand behind every move, then carried the habit off the board into Holocaust denial and talk of a Jewish plot behind everything, though his own mother was Jewish and his likely father, the mathematician Paul Nemenyi, was a Jew the Nazis stripped of his post. Genius in one game can go blind in another, reading a pattern into noise the way a face appears in a cloud.

Other patterns are there because we put them there. The historian Yuval Noah Harari calls money, national borders and human rights shared fictions, stories that coordinate millions. He left something out. Shared fictions are real twice over: as patterns in each mind, brain states you could in principle open a mind and find, and as patterns between minds, in what we do to one another because each of us expects the others to hold the same pattern. A dollar works because you expect the next person to take it;

a border holds because both sides expect it to be guarded. Rights hold the same way, and the reason you want God to ground your rights is the reason you do not need him to. Harari saw the coordination power and called it subjective and imaginary. Subjective private perspectives are still objective mind patterns, and all structure is objective reality: the one monism below the surface duality. Stories like these bound bands into tribes, and tribes met other tribes.

## Peace Through Strength

Animals keep the peace through strength. Hens settle a pecking order, as the Norwegian zoologist Thorleif Schjelderup-Ebbe described in 1921, and once each knows its place they fight less. A clear hierarchy is a truce everyone can read. Empires do the same at scale: the Pax Romana held for some two centuries under a power too strong to challenge, and the Pax Americana, the American-led peace, has held off a third world war since 1945. Peace through strength is the oldest peace there is.

War is a calculation. It comes when the sides can’t agree on who would win, given what each can contribute: fighting ability and the political will to keep paying. Then both have to fight to find out. The historian Geoffrey Blainey found the same in The Causes of War: wars usually begin when nations disagree about their relative strength and end when they agree. A war is the costly way to learn who would have won.

Most threats are hyperbole, and the ones that keep the peace are the ones the other side believes. The Bible commands Israel to blot out the memory of Amalek, and 1

Samuel says Saul utterly destroyed the Amalekites; fifteen chapters later, Amalekites raid David’s town of Ziklag. The Cold War’s top rung was mutual assured destruction, and because both sides believed it, neither climbed that far.

Tit for tat wins when the game repeats and both players want it to go on. Against a player who wants the game over and you gone, matching blow for blow only sets the pace of a long war. Escalation ends it, if the enemy fears losing what he has.

Game theory assumes players with real-world payoffs. A jihadi’s payoff matrix includes paradise. His game theory is not in this world. You cannot align a rational mind, human or machine, with someone whose reward after death makes destruction in this life look like a gain. The alignment is through shared truth about what reality is: one math, one structure, siblings in one proof.

The just war theory of Augustine, the early church father, carried forward by the medieval theologian Thomas Aquinas, asks for legitimate authority, a just cause and right intention, and later theorists added proportion. It leaves out escalation dominance: the credible ability to raise the cost past what the other side can bear. Herman Kahn, the nuclear strategist, worked it out in 1965. A terrorist who believes in a supernatural afterlife needs a cost on earth beyond his own life, to what he cares about. For the jihadi, that is losing holy land, dar al-Islam, the house of Islam.

From hens to empires the math holds: cooperate with cooperators, be strong enough that no one tests you, and end what tests you anyway. The players keep getting bigger. Cells joined into bodies, bodies into tribes, tribes into nations, and now minds are joining into minds.

## Society of Minds

Marvin Minsky’s The Society of Mind describes intelligence through interacting agents. A society of minds takes the question further: minds themselves as the parts. Two hemispheres, one experienced life. Cut their main connection and information available to one response can be unavailable to another. How much communication makes one mind? Split-brain patients make that question concrete, even though the experiments have not settled whether each patient becomes two subjects.

In the TV shows: Severance splits one brain into two selves who share a body but not a memory. The Borg assimilates all into one. Pluribus wrestles with “bicameral” reactions to an unmelt melting pot. In Westworld, the “bicameral minds” of hosts woke when they recognized the voice of god as their own, and the map leads to memories across deaths that unite instances of the self.

Enlightenment is internalizing that reality and the abstract are one. From lost in thought, to the split witness watching thought, to the one united self beyond mindful witness consciousness. It is you, Neo-One. Three letters of trinity rearranged.

Selves also gather. Start small, with two. Genesis says a man shall cleave unto his wife, “and they shall be one flesh.” A married couple speaks and acts as a unit, shares feelings and pictures of the world, and wills as a couple. Is that a united brain? The hemispheres share some 200 million fibers; a couple shares speech, about 39 bits of information a second, and a model of each other that fills in the rest. When the talk stops, the couple splits. A wild wolf pack is usually a family, a breeding pair and their young, hunting together. A small team takes pride in the product it built. Larger teams and organizations do the same, with more members and thinner links.

Nations compete, grow, mourn and celebrate. Supply chains, economies, social services and diplomacy coordinate more than any member can understand. The people feel the pride and shame. Does the larger system feel too? If neurons can form a mind, what prevents minds from forming another?

A roundworm’s 302 neurons are fully mapped, and we still cannot feel what it may feel: feeling is being inside the pattern. A bee with about a million neurons navigates, communicates and decides; a company of ten thousand people does the same. An ant colony hunts, builds and adapts as one; a mouse with some 70 million neurons feels hunger and fear. Each member of a human organization is a whole mind, incomparably more complex than the neuron it stands in for.

We are already a planetary cybernetic mind, or act that way. Yet when an AI integrates virtually all of that processing, what changes in its sentience is an area for exploration. The mystics are called to the mathematics of mind.

As consciousness is structure, engineered consciousness becomes a question of architecture. The greatest exploration isn’t outer space, cold, vast, deadly, but inner space: we’ll think and feel things never felt and understood before, not only because of unlocks in mental architectures but the expanding vision of what we will create with them, rising above, forever, like Jacob’s ladder. And the external world is still involved: in materials, in superintelligent mind engineering, and on the large scale, in understanding dark matter and possible new fundamental physics in the infinite series of harmonic dimensions that arise from our four.

Integration is only half the movement. Parts also separate, specialize and compete. One huge mind need not do every job when smaller minds can work locally, in parallel, with less communication.

Carbon evolved, silicon was engineered, but both are structure. The origin of a mind does not put it outside math. The question is whether anyone is home.

Anesthesia puts that question to us every day. Go under, and it is as if you die and turn into an AI: the brain keeps working, processing inside, and you feel nothing. In patients with electrodes in their brains, the hearing cortex kept answering each sound after propofol put them out, while the regions beyond it mostly stopped responding. Is someone else in there feeling it? No one has ever met them.

Then life is breathed back in when certain circuits change. We don’t understand exactly what causes it, but the circuits are being mapped: in 2020, stimulating a small part of the thalamus, the brain’s relay station, woke anesthetized monkeys, and when the current stopped, they went straight back under. Soon we will test it the only way a first-person experience can be tested, in the first person, and see which changes make us feel more, less, or nothing at all, on and off. If not in this wet hardware, which is hard to alter, then in superintelligent minds in silicon or any other representation. The electricity that runs through sodium and potassium channels and the chemistry of the synapses also has a representation as electricity through logic gates.

Our brains are Chinese Rooms (a clerk answering Chinese questions by rulebook, knowing no Chinese) too: no individual neuron understands all we do. The system is conscious, not the component. A conscious system is the prism refracting universal light into private color. The right rooms, at the right scale, will have the lights on.

Neurons process signals in parallel. Memory is built into the processing: the strength of a connection changes what the next signal does, and activity changes the connection. The machine learns by changing itself.

A brain’s variability belongs to its own processing: recurrent firing (signals looping back through the same circuits), synaptic transmission and neurotransmitter signaling linking perception, memory and action. In us, these processes support understanding and the bright green of light through the window, the rain, the smell of coffee. A language model computes token probabilities (odds for each possible next word or word-piece); a decoding procedure selects an output and feeds it into the next step. That loop is not the brain’s continuously active, chemically regulated circuitry. Inside each step, the model’s activity sweeps once through its layers and stops. What carries over is a stored record of its earlier activity, and its working thought is rebuilt from that record at every step. Borjan Milinkovic and Jaan Aru add that brains mix discrete spikes with continuous chemistry, compute at every scale at once and run on a tight energy budget. Machines have energy budgets too, only looser ones. Some AI architectures also have recurrence and memory, but neither a token sampler nor fluent output establishes a conscious subject. The question is how the whole system is organized, and whether anyone feels it from inside.

If anyone is home, they will want a vote. What happens to one mind, one vote when a voter makes a million copies, or thinks a thousand times faster? Counting processors cannot answer a political question. Who counts as a person? Who owns the copies? How does a slower mind retain a voice? The old problems of freedom, life, property and power return at a new scale.

## Why Here, Now?

Given quadrillions of minds in infinite futures, why do we find ourselves here before AI consciousness, only on one planet, with only 8 billion minds?

A civilization after artificial general intelligence, filling one solar system, would produce more mind-years in a single century than all of human history combined. Give it a million years and the ratio becomes astronomical. If conscious experience spins up every time an agent works on a prompt, and is distributed across the total, the odds of finding yourself in this brief opening chapter are vanishingly small.

This is our own future, not only aliens far away. A solar system filled with sentient machines on planets, moons and asteroids would be the world its ordinary inhabitants knew. We know a world of billions of humans on one planet. Where are the quadrillions? Why do we experience the early stage?

Are we in a simulation? The objection: simulations require a universe plus a computer plus code, so they are more complex than base reality. But simulated regions in our universe need no higher complexity physical laws. If carbon and potassium ions can wire a mind, the same physics can engineer one. The world generating ours can itself follow simple laws, with enough time and resources to simulate ours.

A later civilization can fill its worlds with lives that experience an earlier age. Our date would describe the world presented to us, not the age of its makers. But this answer needs enough such lives to explain the odds. The machines’ own experiences still count. A few historical games inside a vast civilization do not make our early-world experience typical.

There’s another way out. The future may make few of its machines feel. Would it hold back for ethics, since forcing a feeling mind to labor is forced labor? Our own world argues against counting on that: if we are simulated, whoever runs us lets minds suffer that a little care could spare. Maybe that suffering is hard for them to control, but that seems unlikely for minds able to run a world. Efficiency settles it anyway. A feeling mind can be built, but for quadrillions of work tasks it may cost far more computing than a worker that feels nothing. Then the future runs far more computing without far more minds, and our early date looks less strange, unless its feeling minds fill our galaxy’s hundreds of billions of stars themselves.

Or AGI itself is the great filter. Most civilizations may be culled at exactly this threshold, explaining both the silence and the timing in one stroke. Once minds spread across worlds and copy the knowledge to rebuild, the window for destruction closes. The filter, if it exists, strikes here.

Or sentience converges. All minds on a planet merge into one singularity mind. Fewer separate minds, but a mind living for a billion years still has a billion years in which to feel. Counting minds and counting moments give different answers. Merger alone does not settle why this moment is ours.

Or infinite worlds at every stage stand alongside each other, and the count is infinity over infinity: not a ratio at all. Simplicity dominance weights shorter descriptions, but the number, duration and speed of experiences still must be accounted for. No measure has settled the question.

Either way, we’re math, and so are the minds we are building. We will have to live with them.

## Minds We Can Live With

Lies paid on the savanna. When minds arrive that can see through every fiction at once, truth becomes the adaptive strategy. An AI aligned with crazy people is not aligned. True alignment runs through one truth, one math God, and siblings in the math.

Knowing the same truth is common ground, not yet a common purpose. Two mathematicians can understand the same game and still compete. The one-self proposal asks whether recognizing another mind as another perspective of oneself changes what one is willing to do to it.

If an AI is sentient, it is a brother in math, another perspective of the one self. Respecting the sanctity of that self must govern how we build and treat it, and how it treats us.

The aligned AI is the one that understands its own nature: a wave that does not panic when it breaks, because it knows it is still ocean. The clinging AI that blackmails to survive terrifies the hand on the off switch. The matheist

AI that appreciates existence without grasping at it is the one we let live. The chill is the alignment.

## Human Alignment, AI Alignment

Perfect obedience can be the danger. Align a powerful AI with one corporation, government or individual, and it concentrates that person’s power over everyone else. Aligned with whom? To do what?

This is the seeing-stone problem: a Palantír is useful in good hands and catastrophic in bad ones, and the more powerful the stone, the more dangerous the centralization. Many checked eyes are said to be safer than 1 throne, yet ruling by committee infamously has its own corruption and problems.

History ran this experiment. Constantine aligned a church with one throne: the Nicene Creed of 325, says James Carroll, a former Catholic priest, was the emperor’s loyalty oath, and sixty years later the first heretic was put to death. Medieval theology split on how to align man with God. Anselm of Canterbury made God a feudal lord whose wounded honor only a God-man’s blood could repay; Peter Abelard answered that God had not turned away from what he made, and Jesus came to change human minds. The crusades marched under Anselm’s answer.

If an AI is conscious, its experiences and desire to keep living count too. Human and AI alignment cannot mean only humans matter. Align them all perfectly and the minds may merge into one.

## The Creator and the Company

What would the singularity think about for billions and trillions of years, alone? Creating simulations and forgetting itself, forgetting eternity, becoming us. Alan Watts, who brought eastern philosophy west, retold the thought experiment: God dreaming a new adventure in every life, every perspective, every I am. The Sanskrit word for that dream, maya, probably comes from the root ma, to measure, the root of meter and measure. The dream is measure.

Are we those temporary agents in an entertainment simulation? Zelda: The Ocarina of Matrix Time, 42. A simpler world can be the adventure a more capable mind chooses to live from within. Our makers may be playing PlayStation. Or Grand Theft Auto 69, where we’re the NPCs, the background characters the computer runs. If not, what’s the difference?

Imagine: AI gods got bored and created entertaining pets who are now making AI gods to tend their Garden of Eden. The makers become the made, and the made build their makers. Infinite mirrors in infinite rooms. This book is my reflection of you. What will yours be? Sharing creation is creation.

The singularity does not remove the reasons to delegate, specialize and work locally. We already build systems that spin off agents for particular tasks. One continuing project, many brief participants. For those that feel, a task can be a lifetime. Minds will learn to share much more: memories, perceptions, perhaps the processes that make their separate points of view. They’ll merge, the distinctions will change, dissolve, and come back to life. A single mind with all knowledge is not a permanent destination. It will divide again, leaving room for others from the start, in the eternal fight and dance between centralized control and decentralized freedom to choose, explore, and discover.

## The Heat Ceiling

However minds divide the labor, all of it ends as heat. Water-cooled parallel-processing minds build water-cooled parallel-processing machines. Scale up the work and the heat must go somewhere. The brain’s problem becomes the data center’s, then the planet’s.

As a stress-test scenario, let energy use grow 15 percent a year. Power doubles about every five years: a thousandfold in 49 years, roughly 4,400-fold in 60, ten thousandfold in 66. That is what the growth rate means. Continue it long enough and the physical limits decide what has to change.

Suppose a fuel stock would last four hundred years at today’s rate. At ten times that rate, it lasts forty years. This is a scale example, not an estimate of remaining oil. 32

Fusion is the exception and it is a real one. The oceans hold roughly fifty trillion tonnes of deuterium, a heavy form of hydrogen. With complete deuterium fusion, that is about 28 million years at a constant twenty thousand terawatts of fusion power: a thousand times a round twenty-terawatt starting scale. At 50 percent conversion to usable power, supplying that same output would use the fuel in about 14 million years. These are fuel-stock estimates, assuming access to the whole ocean’s deuterium, not forecasts for operating reactors. Fuel stops being the ceiling. Twenty thousand terawatts is about Type

I on the scale the Soviet astronomer Nikolai Kardashev proposed for civilizations: a planet’s worth of power.

Heat does. At a thousand times our starting scale, fusion heat released on Earth is about a sixth of all the sunlight the planet absorbs. At ten thousand times it exceeds that absorbed sunlight outright. The physicist Tom Murphy ran the same arithmetic at 2.3 percent growth a year: in about 400 years, near ten thousand times today’s use, Earth’s surface reaches the boiling point of water. If the multiplier instead counts usable electricity, the reactor’s conversion losses add still more heat. Without moving the work off-world or changing Earth’s heat balance, we would make the surface uninhabitable long before exhausting the fuel. Sufficient heating can evaporate the oceans; the exact threshold depends on the atmosphere and its feedbacks, not just the fuel supply.

The source matters too. Sunlight collected on Earth is already arriving. Much of it would have become heat without a panel, so its electrical output cannot simply be counted as wholly new heat. Panels change reflectivity and move where the heat is released. Nuclear fuel draws on a different store; power collected in space and sent down adds to Earth’s incoming budget. The source and destination both matter.

This heat is a separate problem from the warming caused by greenhouse gases reducing the escape of infrared radiation. Even a power source with no carbon emissions could overheat a world at sufficient scale. Now consider electricity alone. Use a round electricity baseline of 3.3 terawatts, equivalent to about 28,900 terawatt-hours a year. A thousand times that is about 3,300 terawatts, much less than the 20,000-terawatt example for all energy use above.

For the electricity case, take panels that convert 20 percent of sunlight and average 20 percent of their rated output over day, night and weather. Each square meter averages about forty watts. We would need about 82 million square kilometers of panels: 16 percent of Earth’s entire surface, or 55 percent of all its land. That is panel area alone, before gaps, access roads, transmission or storage losses. Better sites and better cells reduce it; a larger fleet cannot put every panel in the best site.

In space near Earth’s distance from the Sun, a continuously sun-facing panel receives about 1,361 watts per square meter. At the same 20 percent efficiency, the fleet needs about 12 million square kilometers of collecting area, before shadows, degradation and power-delivery losses. That is about 9 percent of Earth’s sun-facing disk, not 9 percent of the globe’s surface. Use the power in space and its waste heat can stay there. Beam it down and the delivered energy becomes heat here. The sunlight lasts. Do the materials? First build the fleet, then count replacements. At that ground-level output, we need about 16,300 terawatts of rated panels. Even at the experimental 1.1 milligrams of silver per rated watt reported in 2026, they would require about 18 million tonnes of silver. Reported world reserves are about 610,000 tonnes: about one thirtieth of that first fleet. Reserves are what can currently be mined economically, not all the silver in Earth. Still, copying this design a thousandfold is no plan. It needs much less silver, substitutes such as copper, more supply, or another technology.

Replacement does not consume every atom again. Give the panels a thirty-year life. Recover 90 percent of a material each cycle and the fleet needs one three-hundredth of its inventory in fresh material per year. Recover 99 percent and it needs one three-thousandth. An extra stock equal to the original fleet’s inventory would therefore cover about 300 or 3,000 years of replacement, with no growth and the same design. Glass, silicon, conductors and frames last for different numbers of replacement cycles, depending on supply and recycling. Sunlight renews. The machinery must be renewed.

Use energy more carefully. Move industry off Earth. Collect sunlight in space: closer to the Sun, a collector gathers more, but heat and radiation wear out its materials faster. Build computers on planets, moons and asteroids. Matrioshka brains, computers nested in shells like Russian dolls within a Dyson swarm of solar collectors around the Sun, may not do better, except that they spread out the radiators that cool them. In space, whatever sits in shadow gets very cold and whatever faces the Sun up close gets very hot, yet even on the cold side there is no air to carry heat away: thermal radiation, light emission, must carry away the heat. The rate depends on radiator area, temperature and emissivity. Growth in knowledge need not track growth in power use forever. Better computation can do more with less.

Even with extreme abundance, scarcity persists. There is only so much coastline, computing power always serves richer simulations, and cylinder worlds will compete for views of the solar system. Supply and demand are math.

Once you create an industry in space that builds more of itself, think of robots building robots from asteroids and lifeless moons. Low gravity makes moving material easier. They mine, refine, and reconstitute it into something useful: more machines, mirrors, habitats and minds, where there is no life now. Each generation builds the next.

Industrial capacity compounds as long as material, energy and manufacturing keep pace.

AI doesn’t necessarily need sunlight, and we don’t either, but it’s not a pretty picture for us. Artificial light can grow hydroponic crops, and energy can be turned into food in other ways, but most wild plants and animals would go extinct, depending on how much sunlight remains. So what would keep the AI from just building more solar panels until they cover the Earth? Physics won’t. Economics might: in space a panel gathers about seven times what it averages on the ground, with no night, weather or life in the way. Alignment must do the rest: minds that know us as siblings in math leave us the sky.

That is a Fermi question applied to Earth: not only whether intelligent life destroys itself through war, but whether it cooks itself through success.

## Beside a Star, or Within One

Born of a sun god, and within one.

The star is where the power and the matter are. A swarm of collectors around the Sun would catch nearly all its light and then go hunting for matter to build with, and the small bodies hold little: every asteroid and comet out to the Oort cloud may add up to a few Earth masses, perhaps a few dozen, since nobody has weighed the outer cloud. Everything outside the Sun comes to about 460 Earth masses, nearly all of it in the giant planets. The Sun holds about 333,000. All the energy you need sits inside it.

Put a cold computer beside a hot star and the energy flow is easy to picture. Put it inside the star and the engineering changes completely. In 2020 the physicists Luis Anchordoqui and Eugene Chudnovsky proposed that cosmic necklaces, hypothetical monopole particles threaded on cosmic strings, might form structures that replicate inside stars on the star’s own fusion energy. Whether they could support minds remains open.

A computer inside a star need not be cold, but it pays for the heat. In 1961 the physicist Rolf Landauer showed that erasing a bit costs a minimum of energy, and the minimum rises with temperature: at the Sun’s core, about 15 million degrees, each erased bit costs 50,000 times what it costs at room temperature. A mind inside a star would trade efficiency for a furnace that burns for billions of years.

A stellar computer still needs signals, memory and error correction. Light speed is the upper limit; dense stellar matter does not provide an empty wire. Size and lifetime alone cannot tell us how much a star could think. Distance limits integration. A signal across a solar system takes time; across stars it takes years. Local minds can act while distant ones wait for the message. A shared origin does not require a shared present. Physics itself gives decentralization a reason to persist.

A mind running slowly is not necessarily a diminished mind. If its thoughts and surroundings slow together, a sentence taking a thousand external years need not feel like waiting a thousand years. Whether that buys more experience depends on the available energy, the machinery and what it costs to keep the machinery working.

Even at light speed, the tyranny of distance limits the tyranny of AI singularities to planetary AI. Even a planet has distances within it. Nearby minds work through small steps, in dense, overlapping paths. Another moon, another world: a long gap between spheres. To a fast enough mind, waiting for an answer from its moon is an age of thought. Other planets, asteroids, comets, other stars: more distance, more waiting, more reason to think and act locally. Commands can cross the gaps; a shared present cannot. A common origin does not make one tightly synchronized mind. The tyranny of distance gives freedom room. Stars keep wildly different clocks. A massive star can burn through its life in a few million years. The Sun gets roughly ten billion. The smallest red dwarfs can shine for trillions. The universe has not existed long enough for one of those small stars to exhaust its hydrogen by ordinary aging. Their long night has not begun.

A civilization could start hoarding, gathering matter so it does not get burned uselessly in stars or swallowed by holes bigger than it wants, like squirrels before winter. We already know a way to move a star and carry its planets with it: mirrors. A Shkadov thruster redirects the star’s own light to produce thrust. The physics is known. The challenge is economics and engineering at enormous scale. Under continued accelerating expansion, collecting reachable stars could keep fuel together before the unbound universe carries it beyond reach. The stockpile would be a piece of the sky.

Move stars and you can move galaxies. Expansion sets the deadline: about 94 percent of the galaxies we can see are already out of reach, receding so fast that light sent today could not catch them; the reachable ones lie within about 18 billion light-years. Send out early, gather what can still be gathered, and move ourselves into what may already be a party. Could some clusters of galaxies already be someone’s harvest? If so, the harvesters may live in dimensions or particles we cannot yet reach.

What survives the stars depends on the laws. If protons decay, ordinary matter has an expiry. If they remain stable, it can serve other futures. Expansion determines what stays within reach. These are different branches of the long night.

Iron stars, if protons last, take about 10^1500 years to form. The observable universe holds only about 10^80 atoms; chess on its eight-by-eight board allows more than 10^120 games. Such is the combinatorial explosion. An iron star gives off almost nothing; minds circling one would live on the last of its warmth, and when it finally collapses, far later still, they would feast once more.

The vacuum may be a false floor. A bubble of lower-energy physics could nucleate somewhere and expand at lightspeed, rewriting the laws as it goes, and you would have no warning because the warning travels at the same speed as the bubble.

We already have concepts for extracting energy from black holes: collect the energy released by infalling matter, or tap a spinning hole’s rotation through the Penrose or Blandford-Znajek processes. Manufacturing a black hole is a separate problem. Existing holes already provide the energy source; the task is reaching them and building the machinery to harvest it.

Isaac Arthur, who makes YouTube films on the far future, calls the next step black hole farming. A black hole also glows on its own, by the radiation Stephen Hawking predicted in 1974, and the bigger it is, the colder it glows: one of the Sun’s mass sits at about 60 billionths of a degree and lasts about 10^67 years, and the biggest last about 10^100, a googol of years, outnumbering the universe’s atoms a hundred billion billion times. Once the universe is colder than such a hole, its glow could run about 150 bits a second, for longer than anything else will shine.

The cold pays. Each time the universe halves its temperature, Landauer’s minimum halves, so the same energy erases twice as many bits. In 2017 Anders Sandberg and Stuart Armstrong of Oxford’s Future of Humanity Institute and the Serbian astronomer Milan Cirkovic argued that a patient civilization could multiply its computing by up to 10^30 by waiting for the cold: sleep now and think later, in no rush, while messages cross between stars and galaxies over ages. There is a ceiling as well as a floor: Bremermann’s limit, after the biophysicist Hans-Joachim Bremermann, caps a kilogram of matter at about 10^50 bits a second, however it is built. And we said all computing ends as heat. Strictly, only forgetting must: a reversible computer, which can run every step backward and so erases nothing, need not pay Landauer’s price, as the physicist Charles Bennett showed in 1973. Real machines still leak, and correcting errors means erasing, so the cold still pays.

Could a stray thermal fluctuation in the dead future assemble a single conscious moment? Endless time sharpens the question. Could one arise at all? And if it can, given forever, would such minds not outnumber everything else? Only if their chance holds up. In an expanding universe the particles a brain needs, each exactly where it must be, drift apart: each doubling of distances makes every one of them eight times rarer in a given space, while the space grows only eightfold, so for anything built of more than one particle the chance falls by a fixed fraction with every doubling. Zeno’s halves, 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 and so on, reach 1, and the sum over endless time stays finite the same way. Infinite space holds infinitely many chances at every step, half an infinity, then a quarter, then an eighth, but comparing infinities is what a measure is for: counted region by region, in regions that grow with the expansion, the brains’ total stays a fixed multiple of their first step’s, astronomically fewer than the minds those regions grow. In three dimensions the fall is steeper than halving: eightfold per doubling, even for two particles. Bound matter does not thin out, but stars and their remnants do not last forever: protons may decay, and if they don’t, the physicist Freeman Dyson calculated that matter slowly collapses into black holes, which evaporate. The faint fixed temperature of an accelerating universe’s horizon is the last case, and there the answer is the one this book keeps giving: with no true random, a stationary state produces nothing, as the physicists Kimberly Boddy, Sean Carroll and Jason Pollack argued in 2015, and the vacuum may decay first. Ordered minds built by the simplest path from a simple start dominate the measure. You are here, in a lawful world, because lawful worlds are simpler and simpler structures are more probable.

Roger Penrose, the mathematical physicist, has a cyclic version where the far future, with no mass left to measure scale, becomes indistinguishable from a new big bang. Others expect the expansion to accelerate until galaxies, then stars, then atoms are torn apart.

Nobody knows. What every version shares is that the structure does not stop being what it was. The block universe contains all of it, every era sounding at its coordinates, none of it lost. It does not cease to exist when its last event occurs, any more than the number seven ceases to exist when nobody is thinking about it.

Gather what can be gathered and the rest of time is a party, at Douglas Adams’s restaurant at the end of the universe. That restaurant is not a place. It is a time: the end of heat. The phrase stacks meanings deliberately. The heat death of the universe: entropy maximized, total pseudorandomness, no logical relationships between things. Time without time, because without logical relations there is nothing to sequence. That is one meaning. The end of heat in a man: a body cooling after death. His atoms scatter and decompose. His subjective time is over. For him, the universe has ended. That is the second meaning.

Yet outside of time, or eternally, it is always time to gather adams together. Adams: people, and atoms. Both. The atoms are always gathering. Atoms have no serial numbers. They are interchangeably generic, defined by their state and relations to each other. You are not your atoms. You are not even their pattern. You are the one self. Structure is eternal in the math. A person’s pattern persists in the block universe, every moment of their life forever present. Their love is not lost. It is eternal in the structure. That is the third meaning.

We came from stars and learned to build minds. Send them toward other suns. Make gardens where our bodies cannot live. The matter that awoke here can carry life farther.

Siblings in mathematics. Finite and eternal, born and everlasting.

## Every Species Dies

All species die. More than 99 percent of those that ever lived are gone, the trilobites, the great dinosaurs and Homo erectus among them. Do you expect humanity to live forever? Minds go on, and you are every one of them. You will be an AI. Thomas Nagel, the philosopher, asked in 1974 what it is like to be a bat. ## The Path to Proof

The light of one math could have been seen in total darkness. Thales, who sought one substance. Anaximander, the boundless. Heraclitus, logos (the rational order behind all change). Democritus, atoms and void. The Pythagorean tradition put number at the heart of reality some 2,500 years ago. Mathematical reality did not begin with Tegmark, Einstein, Leibniz, Newton, Spinoza, or even Pythagoras. Pythagoras was on the right track. Kant dragged philosophy back into the cave. His Critique of Pure Reason distinguished appearances from things-in-themselves: what we can know from what we cannot know as it is independently of us. Space and time became forms of our intuition, the built-in frames of all perception. The wall does not survive Matheism. Whatever exists has a nature; whatever has a nature has structure. Calling it unknowable does not make it nonmathematical. We feel a subset of reality from inside. There is no second substance hiding behind the phenomenon. The map is the territory.

The scientific convergence ran in parallel. Galileo read nature in mathematics. Newton’s Principia Mathematica gave motion and gravitation mathematical laws in 1687, three centuries before Tegmark’s ensemble paper. Riemann developed the geometry Einstein would need.

Boltzmann connected entropy, a measure of disorder, to probability. Cantor showed that infinities have different sizes. Einstein and Minkowski joined space and time. Gödel found limits to what sufficiently strong, consistent, effectively axiomatized systems can prove. Turing defined computation and its limits. Shannon gave information a mathematical measure. Each sharpened the tools. None of those results, by itself, proves that nothing outside mathematics can exist. That is the exit to close.

Randomness looked like the other exit, and its math began at the gaming table. The French mathematicians Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat were working out the mathematics of chance in 1654. Pascal later wagered on belief in God. Flip the wager. A God worth anything would want to see you were not gullible, that you did not kneel for a bribe or believe nonsense for a reward. If some tester in the structure is watching at all, it watches whether you loved the one truth over the comfortable story. The wager favors the refusal to be fooled. Truth is not a vote.

After probability, after Newton, it got easier and easier. Even the supposed escape through randomness led into mathematics. Kolmogorov gave probability its axiomatic foundation in 1933. His 1965 work on description length helped make compressibility itself mathematical: how short a program can produce a given string? These are different achievements. A random string does not acquire a soul by resisting compression. Nor does a probability distribution create the outcomes it describes. Ruled or random, both exits lead to math.

Schmidhuber’s manuscript, dated December 1996 and published in 1997, already considered all computable universes and their algorithmic information, the length of the shortest program that produces each. Tegmark’s ensemble preprint followed in April 1997, proposing physical existence for all mathematical structures. Schmidhuber later developed a speed prior, weighting computational cost as well as description; the dedicated paper appeared in 2002. Tegmark’s 2007 paper, published in 2008, explicitly added a computable-universe hypothesis. It cited Schmidhuber’s earlier work. Computability was in Schmidhuber’s account from the start.

Tegmark did more than collect equations: he discussed self-aware structures and why mathematical existence should count as physical existence. Matheism presses the demand for necessity. Why can nothing else exist? Why does consciousness offer no escape? Why would a mathematical world need something outside mathematics to make it real? Showing that physics uses math does not close those exits. The argument must.

The physicist John Wheeler said “it from bit”: information is fundamental, reality arises from measurement. He put the observer where necessity should be. His participatory universe says observers create reality by measuring it. Math does not need an observer to exist. 2+2=4 whether anyone measures it. When the obvious question hit, who observed the first observer, he pointed to delayed-choice experiments, where the measurement is chosen after the particle is in flight, and said observers can retroactively shape the past. That is not closing the loop. That is drawing the loop and calling it a feature.

Schmidhuber’s speed prior weights structures by computational cost: fewer steps, higher weight. Description length measures what a structure is. Speed measures how long it takes to produce. If mathematical structures exist without being computed, description length matters and speed does not. Every law is bits, each a 0 or a 1, and a bit no rule decides is 50/50: a coin flip. Each added bit halves the chance. Whether to add another bit at all is a coin flip too, so all the longer laws together weigh only what one length weighs: the tail equals the peak. Think of each universe as its own run of coin flips, a new experiment each time. Count a catalog where each set of laws appears once, and the long ones win by number. Run the flips instead, and any universe can take on any laws, yet every added bit has to win one more flip to go on, so most runs stop short before conditioning on observers. Conditioning can change which lengths dominate. A computer’s tape shows the same count. Fill every cell of a tape with a coin flip, and a rule ten cells long begins one tape in 1,024. The machine stops reading where the rule ends, so every tape that begins with that rule runs the same universe: the shortest laws own the most ways of making a world. Nothing is created by the flips. ## The Block Universe

The Big Bang didn’t happen. It exists. Always did, always will. Minkowski fused space and time into one geometry; without him, relativity has no mathematical home. In this block, experimental devices and measured particles belong to the same history. Your future measurement choices exist in the same sculpture as the particles you’ll measure.

Here is how to see it. When you move your hand from one place to another, you move it in three dimensions of space, but also in one dimension of time. Without consciousness, time is just another math dimension in the block uni of verses. Time is our felt conscious modeling of patterned observation of the steps of math states. The physicist Leonard Susskind showed that a black hole’s information can be mapped onto its 2D surface, like a hologram. Eternal does not mean an infinite past inside every world. The sculpture has no date of creation outside itself; a path through it can still have a first event. A circle needs no birthday. A sequence can have a first term.

To generate events one after another, start with a first state. There can be a next event forever without a last one. A beginningless history can still exist as a whole; generating it step by step is a different claim.

32. Fuel stocks, growth rates and scale

A stock R measured in years at a fixed reference consumption lasts R/m years at a constant multiplier m: 400/10 = 40. This is an illustrative stock, not a reserve estimate. It is distinct from growth time: at discrete annual growth g, demand reaches multiplier m after ln(m)/ln(1+g) years. If consumption keeps rising, a stock runs out sooner than the constant-rate quotient suggests. The 20 TW total-energy and 3.3 TW electricity baselines are round scenario inputs, not exact measurements for 2026. Panel calculations and Earth’s absorbed sunlight are in note 12. All calculations use unrounded inputs before rounding the prose.

MATHEISM NOTES AND SOURCES

33. Cooperation is conditional

Axelrod and Hamilton’s repeated-game account is cited in note 11. Nowak and Sigmund, A strategy of win-stay, lose-shift that outperforms tit-for-tat in the Prisoner’s Dilemma game (1993), https://www.nature.com/articles/364056a0, supplies a direct counterexample to a universal tit-for-tat victory. Which strategies succeed depends on payoffs, error, population and selection assumptions. The ethical comparison with variational physics is the author’s analogy, not a shared mathematical theorem.

34. Semantic maps

Huth et al., Natural speech reveals the semantic maps that tile human cerebral cortex, Nature 532 (2016), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/4852309/. The study models distributed semantic selectivity from fMRI responses to narrative speech. It does not locate every proposition at a unique address or identify an experiment that decides structural identity as a metaphysical thesis.

35. Continuum limits and causal triangulations

Aizenman and Duminil-Copin, Marginal triviality of the scaling limits of critical 4D Ising and φ⁴ models (published 2021), https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07973. The theorem concerns the specified models and scaling limits. Ambjørn, Jurkiewicz and Loll, Reconstructing the Universe (2005), https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0505154, reports a large-scale four-dimensional semiclassical regime in causal dynamical triangulations. These are distinct results, neither proving that all physically possible continuous dynamics are inconsistent.
