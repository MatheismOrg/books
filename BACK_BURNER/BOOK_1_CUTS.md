Different textual readings became different theology. The church used Greek readings that served its claims about Jesus. Isaiah's young woman, almah, becomes a virgin, though the Great Isaiah Scroll, a Dead Sea Scroll copied about 125 years before Jesus, still says young woman. In Psalm 22, the Hebrew "like a lion" at my hands and feet, among dogs and bulls, competes with the Greek "they dug," traditionally rendered "they pierced." Hebrews turns "I was a husband to them" into "I disregarded them" (8:9), God's faithfulness inverted to justify replacing Israel, and "my ears you have opened" into "a body you have prepared for me" (10:5), a wording used to argue for incarnation. The King James Bible capitalizes Daniel's anointed one into "Messiah the Prince." Yet Daniel does tell of an anointed one cut off, and Isaiah of a servant cut off from the land of the living, whom many Jews read as Israel; the Parables of Enoch, Jewish visions that may predate the gospels, tell of a heavenly Son of Man, God's Anointed One, with the servant's titles: the Chosen One, the Righteous One. The ingredients for a suffering messianic reading were not invented from nothing. The church chose the readings that made him one man.

The big picture is custody. The Dead Sea Scrolls lay hidden in desert caves, outside centuries of Christian copying. They let us test later claims against ancient witnesses the church could not revise. The surviving Septuagint tradition passed through copying, correction and competing editions. Calling a reading ancient does not make it original, and calling a book holy does not make its transmission incorruptible. But buried is not the same as originally error free: the scrolls themselves preserve different Hebrew readings, some agreeing with the Greek against the later Masoretic text. They expose textual variation; they do not certify every Greek difference as Christian fraud.

Psalm 22:17 in Hebrew numbering, 22:16 in many English Bibles, is not a settled prediction of nails. Ka'ari means "like a lion," fitting the poem's threatening animals, though the clause lacks an explicit verb. The Nahal Hever fragment is often read ka'aru. That is not the ordinary Hebrew word for "pierced": connecting it to karu, "they dug," requires accounting for an extra aleph. A confusion between yod and vav is another proposed explanation, not an observed fact about what the scribe intended. The fragment does not settle the original wording. Even the Christian NET Bible defends the lion reading and notes that the New Testament never directly quotes this particular verse about Jesus's death. The garments and the cry of abandonment are clearer textual links.

Nor is "the Septuagint" one untouched volume translated on one date. Jewish translations preceded Christianity; Christian copying and revision continued through the third and fourth centuries CE. Origen's Letter to Africanus describes differences between church copies and Hebrew texts in Daniel, Esther, Job and elsewhere. He defends retaining the churches' scriptures despite those differences. After Origen, Jerome's Prologue to Chronicles describes regional Greek editions associated with Hesychius, Lucian and Origen, the last circulated by Eusebius and Pamphilus. He explicitly describes Origen supplementing the Greek with Theodotion's version and marking the changes. Church custody did not freeze the text. An ancient translation and a later surviving copy are not the same date or the same evidence.

But the piercing interpretation cannot simply be dated after Origen: Justin Martyr already applies it to crucifixion in the second century (Dialogue with Trypho 97). A Christian cannot assume "pierced" proves prophecy, and a mythicist cannot assume it proves that scripture supplied an invented crucifixion. Establish the reading, its date and the direction of borrowing. Changing sides does not repair a weak inference.
