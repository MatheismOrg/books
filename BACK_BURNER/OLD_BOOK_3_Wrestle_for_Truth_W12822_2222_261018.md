## The Passion of the Matheist

The Passion was a genre before it was a gospel. Four centuries before Jesus, the Greek historian Herodotus saw Egyptians at Sais enact by night, on a lake beside the temple, "the story of the god's sufferings, a rite which the Egyptians call the Mysteries" (2.171). The god was Osiris, too holy for Herodotus to mention. Richard Carrier, a historian of early Christianity who puts the odds that Jesus existed at one in three at best, lays out the pattern in a lecture on the mystery cults: in the Mediterranean under the Greeks and Romans, savior gods suffered, won victory over death, and shared it with those initiated into their cults, while China, India and Persia in those centuries grew no such cults. When one region grows the same religion in a dozen local costumes and its neighbors grow none, the parallels mark a trend.

The first god known to die and rise was a goddess. On Sumerian clay tablets from about 1700 BCE, Inanna, queen of heaven, goes down to the land of the dead. At each of its seven gates she gives up a piece of her regalia, her crown, her jewels, at last her robe, until she stands naked before her sister Ereshkigal, queen of the underworld, who fixes on her the eye of death. Her corpse hangs on a hook. After three days and nights her servant goes for help, and Enki, the god of wisdom, sends two creatures who sprinkle the corpse with the food of life and the water of life. Inanna rises. She sends her husband Dumuzi down in her place, and he dies and rises with the seasons. As Tammuz he reached Jerusalem itself: Ezekiel's vision of the temple shows women at its north gate "weeping for Tammuz" (8:14).

Carrier lists others he can document before Christianity. Osiris, killed and cut apart by his brother Set, is put back together by his sister and wife Isis and rules the dead. At Byblos, Lucian, a satirist born in Syria, saw Adonis mourned each year until his worshippers "allege that he is alive again" (On the Syrian Goddess 6). Herodotus tells of Zalmoxis of Thrace, who hid three years in an underground chamber while his people mourned him as dead, and appeared again in the fourth (4.95). In the Roman historian Livy and the Greek biographer Plutarch, Romulus vanishes in a storm and returns from heaven to declare that Rome will be the capital of the world, and Rome worships him as the god Quirinus. Baal dies and returns in the poems of Ugarit, and Carrier traces his cult into the Roman mysteries of Jupiter Dolichenus. Dionysus, the wine god Bacchus, is torn apart and born again. Mithras, the Persian-born god of Rome's soldiers, suffers a passion without dying and wins power over death through it. Carrier, no friend of the church, still throws out the internet's favorite parallel: there was no mystery cult of Horus.

Carrier argues each cult was one Greek frame in local dress: Isis and Osiris for Egypt, Mithras for Persia, Attis and Cybele for Phrygia, Demeter at Eleusis for the Greeks themselves. You were initiated into secrets, the mysteries, by ritual and sworn teaching. You were washed in a baptism, and you ate a sacred meal that reenacted the god's story. You gained brothers and sisters of any race or class, a family you joined by choice instead of birth. The old farm religions had saved the harvest of a whole community; the mysteries turned the yearly rising of the crops into the rising of the individual after death. Carrier sums up the age in four trends: syncretism, henotheism, individualism and cosmopolitanism.

Before the Greeks came the Persians. In Carrier's account, Judaism took from Zoroastrianism the war of a good God against an evil one, light against darkness, Satan and his fallen angels; hell for the wicked and heaven for the good; a river of fire that burns up the world, hell included, before God makes it new; and the resurrection of the righteous to live in it. In Job, the satan is still an officer of God's court, an accuser who comes with the sons of God to present himself before the LORD (1:6).

Elaine Pagels, the Princeton historian of early Christianity, reads what came next as social history: the war in heaven maps a war among people. In the Dead Sea Scrolls, from a sect that broke with the Temple priests and withdrew to the desert, Belial leads the sons of darkness: the Kittim, most likely Rome, and the Jews the sect said broke the covenant. The Gospels inherit the war. In John, Jesus tells Jews who argue with him, "Ye are of your father the devil" (8:44), and Satan enters Judas (13:27). The devil is the rival, projected into heaven.

Carrier calls ancient Judaism henotheist, one God above lesser gods, because angels and demons were called by the same word as gods; what Judaism added, he says, was monolatry, the worship of one. Different Jews believed different things, but the Torah as it stands is strictly monotheist: "See now that I, even I, am he, and there is no god with me" (Deuteronomy 32:39), and the Shema, said morning and evening, declares the LORD one (6:4). Elohim, a plural for gods, may speak of an earlier time, yet from the first line of Genesis it takes a singular verb, and Jews still use it today for strictly one God. A shared word makes no second god: the Torah calls judges elohim (Exodus 22:8), and God tells Moses, "I have made thee a god to Pharaoh" (Exodus 7:1). By the Dead Sea Scrolls, the oldest copied in the third century BCE, the teaching is fixed: the Great Isaiah Scroll reads "I am the LORD, and there is none else" (45:5), and the earliest tefillin yet found, from the caves of Qumran, carry the Shema. Carrier reads the first Christians' Jesus as one of the lesser divine beings, in effect an archangel; the church later made him God, and the Torah had already ruled that out: "God is not a man" (Numbers 23:19).

Dan McClellan, a scholar of the Hebrew Bible, reads the older text as a council of gods. A Dead Sea Scroll of Deuteronomy has the Most High divide the nations "according to the number of the children of God," with Jacob as the LORD's portion (32:8-9), where the standard Hebrew text reads "the children of Israel"; another keeps "bow down to him, all gods" (32:43), which the standard text drops. In Eden God tells the council, "Behold, the man is become as one of us" (Genesis 3:22). Psalm 82 has God judge the gods in their council, "Ye are gods ... But ye shall die like men," and Jesus quoted it to his accusers (John 10:34). McClellan hears "there is none else" as a fan's boast, like a Broncos fan's "there's no other football team." The copies show the direction of travel: one of the gods won out, and the scribes refined the text toward one God. A boast can be rhetoric; a proof cannot: no god made 2 + 2 = 4, and none can unmake it.

Josephus, the Jewish historian of the war with Rome, gives a model closer to home. Some scholars think Mark modeled his trial of Jesus on this one.

A Roman novel has the guarded cross and the missing body. In the Satyricon, credited to Petronius, Nero's arbiter of elegance, though the classicist Ulrike Roth and Walsh place its author in the second-century circle of Pliny the Younger, the Roman senator and letter writer, a soldier guards the crosses of crucified robbers beside a tomb where a widow mourns her husband. He goes in to comfort her, and they lie together for three days behind the tomb's shut doors, while the family of one robber takes his body down. To save the soldier, the widow has her husband's corpse hung on the empty cross, and the next day the people wonder how the dead man got up there (111-112). Classicists count the story among the Milesian tales, bawdy stories older than both Petronius and the Gospels. Matthew posts soldiers at Jesus's tomb and has the priests pay them to say his disciples "came by night, and stole him away while we slept" (28:13).
