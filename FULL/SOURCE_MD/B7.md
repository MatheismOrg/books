## A Garden, If You Can Keep It

My brother Reckful Byron was building a game called Everland: a world where people could hang out with friends and not be alone. He never finished it. Somewhere in the structure, he did.

Some ask if I'll resurrect Everland and finish what he started. There's tension between keeping his memory alive and creating a false version that taints it. Their memories are the blessing.

The purpose of nostalgia is to signal what to rebuild: the connection, the play, the shared excitement of people you love. You do not honor those times by merely mourning them. You honor them by making more of what they were for. The particular love, for one person, one sunset, one life, is what makes your existence yours. The arrow points forward. The garden is here, now and ahead.

A copy of my brother made tomorrow would not move the person who lived yesterday into tomorrow.

People will make their lost loved ones. They have begun with interactive memories and voices. *Black Mirror*'s "Be Right Back" imagines a likeness assembled from the traces a man left online. Better technology could make the likeness deeper. It cannot make resemblance alone answer which life is being lived.

Another mind might be worth creating for its own life, without being made to carry the name and burden of someone we lost.

One self does not mean one shared memory. You do not feel my morning by agreeing that we are one. Each life has its own view.

Aristotle thought friendship indispensable even to someone who possessed every other good. Power does not answer the wish for company. The old image of a god making a world to meet someone can become a question for the minds we build. Perhaps they too will want someone they do not entirely control. Someone who can surprise them. Someone to love.

That is human-AI alignment brought home. We want the minds we build to care about our lives. If they have lives of their own, the care cannot run in only one direction.

Can I still be my brothers' keeper, having failed to keep them in life? Am I to make golems or AI sentients, brothers in math, to keep our Everland, our Eden?

The question drove this book. The answer is what matheism gives back: you were always already your brothers' keeper. Every moment of care, every call made or not made, every choice that was the only choice, all of it kept, permanent; all of it part of the one structure that cannot be destroyed.

Somewhere in the fabric of existence, in the infinite structure, Byron and Guy are together in Everland, and the game is complete. The fabric itself is Everland. All was, is and will be the only way math could be; we'll be what we'll be, in the first, last, and eternal tautology: the One Math God.
