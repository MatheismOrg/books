### The Passion

The Passion was a genre before it was a gospel. Four centuries before Jesus, the Greek historian Herodotus saw Egyptians at Sais enact by night "the story of the god's sufferings, a rite which the Egyptians call the Mysteries" (2.171). The god was Osiris, too holy for Herodotus to mention. Richard Carrier, a historian of early Christianity and a mythicist, who puts the odds that Jesus existed at one in three at best, lays out the pattern in a lecture on the mystery cults: in the Mediterranean under the Greeks and Romans, savior gods suffered, won victory over death, and shared it with those initiated into their cults, while China, India and Persia in those centuries grew no such cults. When one region grows the same religion in a dozen local costumes and its neighbors grow none, the parallels mark a trend.

The first god known to die and rise was a goddess. On Sumerian clay tablets from about 1700 BCE, Inanna, queen of heaven, goes down to the land of the dead. At each of its seven gates she gives up a piece of her regalia, her crown, her jewels, at last her robe, until she stands naked before her sister Ereshkigal, queen of the underworld, who fixes on her the eye of death. Her corpse hangs on a hook. After three days and nights her servant goes for help, and Enki, the god of wisdom, sends two creatures who sprinkle the corpse with the food of life and the water of life. Inanna rises. She sends her husband Dumuzi down in her place, and he dies and rises with the seasons. As Tammuz he reached Jerusalem itself: Ezekiel sees women at the temple "weeping for Tammuz" (8:14).

Carrier lists others he can document before Christianity. Osiris, killed and cut apart by his brother Set, is put back together by his sister and wife Isis and rules the dead. Lucian, a satirist born in Syria, saw Adonis mourned each year until his worshippers "allege that he is alive again" (On the Syrian Goddess 6). Herodotus tells of Zalmoxis of Thrace, who hid three years in an underground chamber while his people mourned him as dead, and appeared again in the fourth. In the Roman historian Livy and the Greek biographer Plutarch, Romulus vanishes in a storm and returns from heaven to declare that Rome will be the capital of the world, and Rome worships him as the god Quirinus. Baal dies and returns in the poems of Ugarit. Dionysus, the wine god Bacchus, is torn apart and born again. Mithras, the Persian-born god of Rome's soldiers, suffers a passion without dying and wins power over death through it. Carrier, no friend of the church, still throws out the internet's favorite parallel: there was no mystery cult of Horus.

Carrier argues each cult was one Greek frame in local dress: Isis and Osiris for Egypt, Mithras for Persia, Attis and Cybele for Phrygia, Demeter at Eleusis for the Greeks themselves. You were initiated into secrets, the mysteries, by ritual and sworn teaching. You were washed in a baptism, and you ate a sacred meal that reenacted the god's story. You gained brothers and sisters of any race or class, a family you joined by choice instead of birth. The old farm religions had saved the harvest of a whole community; the mysteries turned the yearly rising of the crops into the rising of the individual after death. Carrier sums up the age in four trends: syncretism, henotheism, individualism and cosmopolitanism.

Before the Greeks came the Persians.
